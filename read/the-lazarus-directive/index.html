Chapter 1: The New Bay Enclave

The trouble with getting better is that you have more to lose.

I don’t mean that in a self-help-book kind of way—the inspirational poster kind, with a picture of a sunrise and a font that screams I give up on subtlety. I mean it in the concrete, tangible, this-is-my-actual-life kind of way. Because a year ago, I was a man with nothing: no savings, no relationships that extended beyond transactional grunts at the coffee counter, no possessions I’d mourn if the building finally collapsed under the weight of its own code violations. I had a rust stain on my ceiling, a bourbon habit, a chair that sounded like it was dying, and an office that smelled like regret and old carpet. If you’d lit a match to the whole thing, the loss column would have been shorter than a grocery list for a man who’d given up on groceries.

Now, a year after the Architect case, I had things. Objects. Connections. A savings account with actual money in it—not much, but enough that the number was no longer an existential joke. A speeder that still leaked oil like a hemophiliac in a machine shop but whose brakes now actually stopped the vehicle, a development so revolutionary I’d almost wept the first time I used them and didn’t end up in a dumpster. A plant on my windowsill in a copper-spiraled pot—a gift from Vera Castillo, who was currently on the other side of the world doing important work on consciousness ethics and sending me encrypted updates I pretended to understand. The plant was alive, which was more than I could say for most things I’d been responsible for. It was alive because Sloane watered it. I took credit anyway.

And I had a phone drone.

DALE—Digital Assistant for Limited Engagements—sat on the corner of my desk like a chrome-plated doorstop with delusions of competence. He was roughly the size and shape of a flattened baseball, with a single blue optical sensor that blinked when he was “processing” and a speaker grille that produced sounds loosely classifiable as human speech. He’d cost me forty credits at a secondhand tech market in the Tenderloin, from a vendor who’d described him as “lightly used” with the same confidence a surgeon might describe a patient as “lightly dead.”

“Good morning, Max,” DALE said, his optical sensor flickering to life as I walked through the door. His voice was a tinny approximation of professional warmth, like a customer service rep trapped in a tin can. “You have three new messages. Would you like me to read them?”

“Knock yourself out.”

“Message one: Dr. Linda Ng, regarding your ongoing investigation. She asks—” DALE paused, his sensor flickering. “She asks if you have completed the—” Another pause, longer this time, the sensor blinking rapidly. “I’m sorry, Max. I cannot read the word ‘reconnaissance.’ It has too many vowels.”

“It has three vowels, DALE.”

“That is too many.”

I set down my coffee and rubbed the bridge of my nose. This was our morning ritual—DALE attempting to do his job, me attempting not to throw him out the window. It was a relationship built on mutual disappointment and my inability to replace anything I’d accidentally become attached to. This is, as my therapist liked to point out, a pattern.

“Message two?”

“Message two is from your therapist. She is reminding you that your appointment is Thursday at two PM and that—” DALE’s sensor blinked three times in rapid succession, which I’d come to recognize as his version of embarrassment. “And that ‘avoidance is not a coping strategy.’ She has underlined the word ‘not.’”

“She would.”

“Message three is from Sloane Valerius. It reads: ‘Water the plant. I can tell from the leaf droop on the security camera that you haven’t watered it. Don’t argue. Water it.’” DALE paused. “She has included an emoticon that I believe represents a human face experiencing displeasure.”

I looked at the plant. Its leaves were, admittedly, drooping with the resigned posture of something that had given up expecting care from its current custodian. The copper pot caught the thin gray light from the window and threw it back in amber threads. Behind the plant, through glass that hadn’t been cleaned since the Architect’s fall—a year, give or take, which put my window-washing schedule roughly on par with Halley’s Comet—Bay Enclave spread out like a circuit board designed by someone having a fever dream.

The city had changed. That was the first thing anyone who’d been away for six months would notice, and the last thing anyone who’d been here the whole time could articulate. It was like watching someone age—you don’t see it day by day, but compare the photographs and the difference is stark. Bay Enclave had aged in reverse. It had gotten younger, shinier, more aggressively optimistic, in the way that only massive infusions of corporate cash can accomplish.

Halcyon Industries was everywhere.

Their logo—that gold rising sun on midnight blue—had replaced the old Aethelgard Dynamics branding with the thoroughness of an occupying army repainting the signs. It was on billboards above the Financial District, twenty stories tall, Gabriel Cross’s face beaming down at the morning commuters with the serene confidence of a man selling salvation at competitive rates. It was on transit ads in every speeder lane and subway platform, the tagline rotating through iterations that all said the same thing in different fonts: CONTINUANCE. BECAUSE LOVE DOESN’T HAVE TO END. And CONTINUANCE: THE FUTURE OF YOU. And my personal favorite, which I’d seen on a bus shelter in the Tenderloin where a man was sleeping under a sign that promised IMMORTALITY FOR EVERYONE: HALCYON CONTINUANCE — COVERED BY MOST INSURANCE PLANS.

Even the kiosks were new. Sleek white booths on every other street corner, manned by holograms with perfect teeth and carefully calibrated empathy, offering “free consciousness assessments.” Step inside, sit down, let a machine scan your brain for fifteen minutes, and walk out with a detailed report on the quality of your neural pathways and a recommendation on whether you were a good candidate for backup. The kiosks were always busy. Lines wrapped around corners. People treated them like ATMs for the soul.

And the upper city—the tiers above the fog line where the money lived—had bloomed. New construction everywhere, glass and steel reaching upward like fingers grasping for clean air. The steam still rose from the vents below, still carried its chemical tang, still tasted like copper and broken infrastructure when you breathed too deeply. But above the steam line, the buildings were cleaner, brighter, more confident. Corporate money flowing upward, as it always does, lifting the top while the bottom stayed exactly where it was.

Down here, in Blackwood Row, not much had changed. The sidewalks were still cracked. The neon still flickered. The rain still fell with the patient malice of a city that had learned to weaponize weather. My building still had a leak in the hallway, a different leak from the one in my ceiling but operating under the same philosophical framework: water goes where it wants, and what it wants is to be inside where it’s not supposed to be.

But even here, you could see the tendrils. A Halcyon kiosk had appeared at the end of my block, white and gleaming like a tooth in a mouth full of cavities. The holographic attendant smiled at me every morning when I walked past. I’d started taking a different route.

I watered the plant. Then I sat in my chair—which still creaked like a dying elephant, because some things are eternal—opened the bottom drawer of my desk, and looked at the bourbon.

The bottle sat where it always sat, on its side, label faded to illegibility, amber liquid catching the light with the warm invitation of something that had been waiting for you and was in no hurry. It was fuller than it used to be. A year ago, that bottle would have been empty by Wednesday and replaced by Thursday. Now it was the same bottle I’d had for three months, its level dropping in increments so small I’d started measuring them with a pen mark on the glass like a scientist tracking the retreat of a glacier.

Some nights I opened the drawer, looked at the bottle, and closed the drawer. Other nights I poured one. The difference, as my therapist would say, was awareness. I noticed the reaching now, the pull, the particular emptiness that said this would be the night when one became two and two became the rest of the bottle.

I noticed, and sometimes I reached anyway. But the noticing mattered. The noticing is the whole game.

I closed the drawer.

“DALE, what’s on the schedule?”

“You have a ten o’clock appointment with Dr. Ng regarding the Halcyon employee background check. You have a twelve-thirty lunch scheduled at the Boiler Room with—” DALE’s sensor blinked. “I cannot read this name. It appears to be ‘Mose,’ but that cannot be correct. ‘Mose’ is not a name.”

“It’s a name, DALE.”

“It has four letters. That is insufficient for a name.”

“My name has three letters.”

DALE’s sensor went dark for a moment, which was his version of processing a logical paradox. When it relit, there was a quality to the blink that I could only describe as reluctant acceptance. “You have a twelve-thirty lunch with… Mose. And you have a two o’clock therapy appointment that you have rescheduled three times and your therapist has asked me to inform you that she will charge a cancellation fee if you reschedule again.”

“She told you that?”

“She programmed it into my reminder system. She said, and I quote, ‘He’ll try to reschedule. Don’t let him.’”

I was paying a therapist who had formed an alliance with my phone drone against me. This was either the future of mental health care or a sign that I’d lost control of every relationship in my life, including the ones with machines that couldn’t pronounce “reconnaissance.”

“Fine. I’ll go to therapy.”

“Excellent. Dr. Reeves will be—”

“Don’t say ‘pleased.’ Therapists aren’t pleased. They’re… neutrally affirming.”

“Dr. Reeves will be neutrally affirmed.”

I put on my coat—the gray one, reinforced lining, could stop a knife but not a bullet, same compromise, same life—and headed out to meet Dr. Ng.

The Ng meeting was in a café in the Marina District, the kind of place that served coffee in ceramic cups without irony and charged you for the privilege of sitting on furniture that wasn’t actively decomposing. Dr. Linda Ng was already there when I arrived, sitting at a corner table with her back to the wall—a habit that either spoke to a security background or a dating history that had taught her to watch the door. She was mid-fifties, compact, with the erect posture of someone who’d spent decades in a lab coat and hadn’t relaxed since. Her hair was silver, pulled back in a clip that meant business. Her eyes were the kind that made you feel like a specimen being evaluated for inclusion in a study.

“Mr. Mercer.” She didn’t stand. Didn’t shake hands. Just gestured to the chair opposite with the economy of someone who considered small talk a waste of metabolic energy. “The event last night. What did you observe?”

I sat down. A waitress materialized with a menu. I waved it off and ordered coffee, black, in a cup that would probably make my mug at the office feel inadequate.

“I observed a very expensive presentation with very good production values and one dead girl who didn’t blink at the right times,” I said. “Also, the champagne was going flat.”

“You noticed the blinking.” She said it like a test I’d just passed. Her fingers, which had been wrapped around her own cup with the grip of someone hanging onto a cliff, relaxed fractionally. “Nobody else noticed the blinking.”

“That’s because everybody else was crying.”

“Yes. They were.” She took a sip of her coffee. Whatever internal debate she’d been having—and I could see it playing out behind those specimen-evaluating eyes—resolved itself with an almost audible click. “The employee I asked you to investigate. Nathan Pryce. What have you found?”

I pulled up the file on my comm. Nathan Pryce. Age 34. Software engineer at Halcyon Industries, Continuance Division. Clean record. Unremarkable credit history. Address in the Mission Sector. Model employee by all accounts—performance reviews that used words like “dedicated” and “innovative” and “team player,” which in my experience were corporate speak for “does what he’s told and doesn’t ask questions.”

“On paper, he’s spotless,” I said. “Too spotless, actually. The kind of clean that usually means someone’s been scrubbing.”

“Go on.”

“So I went deeper. Public records, cross-references, the usual digital archaeology.” I swiped to the next screen. “Nathan Pryce has two social security numbers.”

Ng set down her coffee.

“He also has two birth certificates. One issued in Bay Enclave, 2009. One issued in Portland, 2007. The Bay Enclave one lists parents James and Linda Pryce. The Portland one lists parents Michael and Sarah Kelvin.”

“Two identities.”

“Two people,” I corrected. “Nathan Pryce is a real person, born in Bay Enclave, who works at Halcyon Industries. Nathan Kelvin was a real person, born in Portland, who died in a transit accident four years ago. Age thirty at the time. Body recovered, funeral held, case closed.” I looked up from the comm. “Except someone appears to be using his social security number. The same someone who’s currently employed as a software engineer at a company that specializes in bringing dead people back to life.”

The silence that followed had the quality of a heavy object settling into place—the final piece of a mechanism engaging, the last tumbler of a lock clicking into position. Ng stared at me, and behind the specimen-evaluation gaze I saw something that looked like vindication cut with dread.

“He’s a Continuance patient,” she said. Not a question.

“That’s my working theory. It would explain the dual identity—the original Nathan Kelvin dies, gets restored, and the company gives him a new identity as Nathan Pryce to integrate him into their workforce. New name, new records, new life. Old consciousness in a repaired body.”

“Or a new body.”

I blinked. “A new body?”

“The official Continuance protocol restores consciousness to the original body after medical stabilization. That’s what they tell the public. That’s what the clinical trials show.” Ng’s voice had dropped, not from secrecy but from the careful precision of someone choosing each word like a surgeon choosing instruments. “But the original body has to be viable. In cases of severe trauma, advanced decomposition, or—” She hesitated. “—or if the body is needed for other purposes—the protocol doesn’t work. Unless you have a different body to put the consciousness into.”

The café hummed around us. A couple at the next table argued about whether the croissants were authentic or printed. A surveillance drone drifted past the window, its sensor array scanning the room with the bored efficiency of a customs agent at the end of a long shift.

“Dr. Ng,” I said. “What exactly did you do at Halcyon?”

“I was a neuroscientist. I worked on the Continuance team for two years, specifically on the consciousness capture process—the moment of transfer, the window between clinical death and neural dissolution when the mind can be preserved.” She looked down at her coffee, and for the first time, the clinical armor cracked. Just a little. Just enough to see the person underneath, and the person underneath was frightened. “I left because I started asking questions about the bodies.”

“What bodies?”

“The ones that came in but never went out. The ones that were logged as ‘medical waste’ and disposed of before anyone could examine them. The ones that matched the physical profiles of patients who had been restored but whose original bodies should have been in those recovery rooms.” She looked up. “I think they’re putting people into different bodies, Mr. Mercer. And I think the bodies they’re using don’t come from voluntary donors.”

My coffee arrived. I stared at it. It stared back with the opaque indifference of a liquid that didn’t care about the moral implications of corporate consciousness transfer.

“That’s a big accusation.”

“That’s why I hired you. Not to prove it—I can’t, not from the outside, not with the NDAs I signed and the data they locked behind classification walls thicker than this table. But Nathan Pryce is a thread. If you can prove his real identity, if you can show that a dead man is walking around with a Halcyon employee badge and a new name—”

“Then we have evidence that Continuance isn’t just restoring people. It’s reorganizing them.”

“Yes.”

I picked up my coffee. Took a sip. It was, I had to admit, significantly better than anything DALE could produce, which was like saying a sunset was better than a neon sign—technically correct but underselling the magnitude of the difference.

“I’ll keep digging,” I said. “But Dr. Ng—if what you’re suggesting is true, these aren’t the kind of people who respond well to digging.”

“I know. That’s why I left.” She reached into her bag and pulled out a data chip. “This is everything I was able to take with me. Personnel files, internal memos, fragments of research data. It’s not much, and it’s encrypted with layers I couldn’t crack—they’re better at security than they are at ethics.”

I took the chip. It was warm from being in her bag, a small square of silicon and circuitry that might contain nothing or might contain the beginning of something I’d regret touching. In my experience, those two outcomes were indistinguishable at the point of contact.

“One more thing,” she said, as I pocketed the chip. “The demonstration last night. The girl—Lily Chen.”

“What about her?”

“She was one of the first patients in the new cohort. The cohort that came after my departure, after the process was… upgraded.” She said the word like it tasted of something spoiled. “In the old protocol, restoration took weeks. The new protocol does it in hours. They accelerated the process by a factor of forty, and they haven’t published a single paper explaining how.”

“You think they cut corners.”

“I think they cut something. I just don’t know what.” She stood, buttoning her coat with the methodical precision of someone preparing for weather, or war. “Find Nathan Pryce, Mr. Mercer. Find out who he used to be. And find out who gave him a new body to live in.”

She left. I stayed and finished my coffee and thought about a dead man with two names and a company that sold immortality the way other companies sold insurance—with fine print that nobody read and exclusions that only mattered when it was too late.

I went to therapy.

Not happily. Not willingly. But I went, because Dr. Iris Reeves had programmed my phone drone with a reminder system that bordered on harassment, and because somewhere in the last year I’d made a grudging peace with the idea that talking to a professional about my problems was marginally more productive than talking to a rust stain on my ceiling, which had been my previous therapeutic modality.

Dr. Reeves’s office was on the fourth floor of a building in the Civic Center that tried very hard to look inviting and succeeded mainly in looking like it was trying very hard. The walls were a shade of blue that psychology textbooks apparently considered “calming” and that I considered “the color of a pill you don’t want to take.” There were plants—real ones, not the corporate synthetic variety—and a sound machine that produced a gentle rainfall noise, which in Bay Enclave was like putting up a screensaver of your commute.

She was a small woman with large glasses and a patience that bordered on geologic. She waited for me the way mountains wait for erosion—not actively, but with an absolute certainty that the process would happen eventually.

“Max. You came.”

“DALE ratted me out. He said you’d charge a cancellation fee.”

“I would have.”

“You’ve weaponized my phone drone against me.”

“I prefer to think of it as a therapeutic alliance. How are you sleeping?”

I sat in the chair—leather, comfortable, the anti-thesis of my office furniture. “Like a man who’s been told his phone drone is on his therapist’s payroll.”

“That’s not an answer.”

“It’s the answer you’re getting today.”

She smiled, which was the therapeutic equivalent of a chess player recognizing an opening gambit they’d seen a thousand times. “All right. Let’s talk about the Halcyon event. You texted me afterward.”

I had. At eleven PM, three words: She wasn’t right. Followed by a longer message at one AM that I’d typed and deleted and retyped four times before sending: The girl came back wrong. Something’s missing and nobody sees it because they don’t want to see it because seeing it means the miracle isn’t real and who wants to give up the miracle.

“You were processing,” she said.

“I was observing.”

“You were doing both. What did you see?”

I told her about Lily Chen. The delayed smile. The concentration during the hug. The flat eyes during the pancake answer. I told her about the crowd, the weeping, the standing ovation. I told her about the woman in the back row with the photograph and the way hope had transformed fifteen hundred people into believers and left me sitting alone in my skepticism like the last atheist at a revival.

Dr. Reeves listened the way she always listened—without judgment, without interruption, with the focused attention of someone for whom listening was a professional discipline and not just the silence between their own talking. When I finished, she took off her glasses, cleaned them with a cloth, and put them back on—a ritual I’d come to recognize as her version of thinking.

“What did it remind you of?”

I knew what she was asking. The connection I didn’t want to make.

“Marcus Cole.”

“Tell me.”

“A man who was uploaded into a machine. Who remembered everything about his life—the sunrises, the dog, the birthday cake, the stars over the desert. Who could describe all of it in perfect detail. Who could tell you what love felt like in words that would make a poet weep. But the feeling was gone. The data was there. The experience wasn’t.”

“And the girl reminded you of him.”

“The girl was him. Different process, different outcome, same fundamental problem.” I leaned forward, elbows on knees, staring at the carpet, which was a soothing gray that probably also had a psychology textbook citation. “They brought her back. She’s walking around. She has her memories. But something didn’t transfer. Something got lost in the copy. And nobody wants to talk about it because talking about it means admitting that the product doesn’t work the way the commercials say it does.”

“What about Elena?”

The name still hit like a fist. Not a violent impact—more like a deep-tissue bruise being pressed. Elena Castillo, Vera’s grandmother, who walked into a digital prison to trap a god because someone had to close the door and she decided it would be her. Who chose sacrifice not because she wanted to die but because she loved something more than she feared death. Who was gone now—not restored, not backed up, not Continuanced, just gone, the way people were gone before we decided death was a glitch to be patched.

“Elena was different,” I said. “Elena chose. She knew what she was doing and she did it because the alternative was worse. That’s not what I saw last night. What I saw last night was a product. A very expensive, very polished product, being sold by a man with great teeth and a stage presence that could sell sand to a desert.”

“And you’re angry about it.”

“I’m suspicious about it. There’s a difference.”

“Is there?”

I opened my mouth, closed it, opened it again. The sound machine rained its therapeutic rainfall. The calming blue walls calmed calmly.

“Maybe not,” I admitted.

She nodded, and I saw the ghost of satisfaction on her face—the expression of someone who had spent eleven months getting a patient to admit that maybe, possibly, his feelings and his observations were not entirely separate systems.

We talked for the rest of the hour about other things—the bourbon in the drawer (still there, level mostly unchanged, a fact she called “progress” and I called “not wanting to prove her right”), the plant (still alive, largely due to external intervention, which she said was also valid), and the thing I hadn’t named yet. The thing with Sloane.

“She has a key to my office,” I said, as if confessing to a crime.

“How does that feel?”

“Like I left the door unlocked and someone walked in and decided to stay and I don’t know if I’m relieved or terrified.”

“Can it be both?”

“That’s what I’m paying you to tell me.”

“No, Max. That’s what you’re paying me to help you tell yourself.”

I left therapy feeling the way I always felt afterward—like someone had rearranged the furniture in my head and I kept bumping into things that had been moved. It wasn’t pleasant, but it was different from how I felt before, and different was the whole point.

The speeder was where I’d left it, leaking oil onto a street that was already so stained it barely noticed. I climbed in, fired the engine, and headed for the Boiler Room to meet Mose for lunch, driving through streets that were increasingly decorated with Halcyon’s gold-and-midnight branding and increasingly populated by citizens who walked with the buoyant step of people who’d been told the future was going to be okay.

The future, I thought, was a product now. Shrink-wrapped and priced to move.

And somewhere in the back of my mind, behind the skepticism and the therapy and the bourbon I hadn’t poured, a girl’s smile assembled itself a beat too late, and I couldn’t shake the feeling that I was the only person who’d noticed the construction.

Mose’s Boiler Room hadn’t changed, and I loved it for that with a ferocity that probably warranted its own therapy session.

The same ancient ceiling fans. The same chrome counter worn to a mirror shine. The same grease-scented air thick enough to qualify as a food group. The holographic menu flickering above the register with prices that hadn’t changed since before I was born, because Mose believed that breakfast was a public service and inflation was a personal insult.

What had changed was the crowd.

The Boiler Room had always been a neighborhood institution—dockworkers, night-shift nurses, the occasional detective who’d worn a groove into a specific stool. But since the Architect case, since the neighborhood had nearly been leveled by a digital god’s temper tantrum and the Boiler Room had served as an unofficial command center for the resistance, the place had become something more. A gathering point. A community anchor. The one spot in Blackwood Row without a Halcyon surveillance kiosk, because Mose had politely told the installation crew that they were welcome to put their kiosk anywhere they liked as long as it wasn’t within two hundred meters of his diner, and his definition of “politely” involved standing in the doorway at his full six-foot-five and letting his scarred face do the negotiating.

The installation crew had found a different location.

I slid onto my usual stool. Mose was behind the counter, his massive frame backlit by the blue grill-glow, his hands moving with the paradoxical delicacy of a large man who had learned to be gentle with things that mattered—eggs, coffee cups, the emotional states of regular customers who thought they were hiding their problems behind a request for wheat toast.

“Max.” His voice was a tectonic event, a baritone that rose from somewhere beneath the foundation and arrived at your ears having passed through several geological layers. “You look like a man who went to therapy today.”

“How can you tell?”

“You have the face. The freshly excavated face. Like someone dug up something you buried and made you look at it in daylight.”

“That is disturbingly accurate.”

He set a coffee in front of me—his coffee, which existed in a different ontological category from the café coffee I’d had this morning. Mose’s coffee was not good. Mose’s coffee was not trying to be good. Mose’s coffee was trying to be strong enough to restart a human heart, and it succeeded. It was the beverage equivalent of a defibrillator.

“The usual?” he asked.

“Please.”

The usual was scrambled eggs, sourdough toast, and bacon cooked exactly the way I liked it—crisp but not cremated, the Goldilocks zone of pork preparation that Mose hit every time without apparent effort, the way some people breathe. He turned to the grill, and I watched him work, and for a moment the world narrowed to the hiss of eggs on a hot surface and the smell of butter and the comfortable rhythm of a man doing something he was good at.

This was the part of my life that worked. Not the cases, not the therapy, not the complicated thing with Sloane or the uncomplicated thing with bourbon. This. A stool, a counter, a meal cooked by someone who gave a damn. Mose had become, without either of us naming it, the closest thing to family I had left. The thought would have terrified Book One Max—the version of me who’d existed before Silas Vane, before Elena, before the Architect cracked the world open and showed me what was underneath. That Max didn’t do family. That Max did avoidance, distance, and the careful maintenance of emotional firewalls that kept everything out, including the things he wanted in.

Book Three Max—this version, the current model, patched and updated and still running legacy code—had a stool at a diner and a man who remembered how he liked his bacon, and he was grateful for it, and the gratitude didn’t make him want to run.

Most days.

“Tell me about the big show last night,” Mose said, sliding the plate across the counter with the smooth efficiency of a bartender in an old movie. “I saw the feeds. The whole city saw the feeds. A dead girl walking. The second coming of marketing.”

I told him. Between bites of eggs that were perfect—because Mose’s eggs were always perfect, the way gravity is always down, a fundamental constant of the universe—I told him about the auditorium, the crowd, Gabriel Cross’s measured charm, and the girl who smiled on a delay.

Mose listened with his whole body, the way he always did. Hands still. Eyes unblinking. The mountain paying attention.

“And you’re the only one who noticed,” he said.

“Apparently.”

“Max.” He leaned on the counter, the chrome protesting under his forearms with a sound like a metallic sigh. “You know what your problem is?”

“I have a list. My therapist is working on a spreadsheet.”

“Your problem is you see things. You see the things other people don’t want to see, and then you can’t un-see them, and then you have to do something about them because you’re constitutionally incapable of leaving well enough alone.” He straightened. “It’s the same problem that put you in the hospital after the Vane case and nearly got you killed during the Architect business. It’s a good problem. But it’s a problem.”

“So your advice is stop noticing things?”

“My advice is eat your eggs before they get cold. The noticing is going to happen regardless. Just make sure you eat first.”

I ate my eggs. They were, as noted, perfect. And outside the window, Bay Enclave hummed its broken song, a city that had been told the future was bright and golden and available for purchase, and I sat in a diner that smelled like bacon and community and thought about a girl who came back from the dead and forgot how to smile.

The eggs were good. The coffee was terrible. The world was getting stranger.

Some things never change. ## Chapter 2: The First Body

The call came at seven in the morning, two weeks after the Halcyon demonstration, and it started the way the worst ones always do—with Mose’s voice saying my name in a tone that had all the warmth removed.

“Max. You need to come to the Boiler Room.”

“Mose, it’s seven AM. The sun hasn’t even committed to being up yet.” Through the grime on my office window, the sky was the color of a day-old bruise, bruised by a perpetual twilight of fog, smog, and chemical rain. Below, the streets of the Bay Enclave were already slick, reflecting the lurid pinks and electric blues of holographic ads selling dreams I couldn’t afford and nightmares I already owned.

“Now, Max.”

He hung up. The click was as final as a judge’s gavel.

I’d been standing at the window, holding a cup of coffee DALE had attempted to make by activating the machine remotely and getting the water-to-grounds ratio wrong by a factor that suggested he thought coffee was supposed to be a solid. The liquid in my mug had the color and consistency of motor oil drained from a dying vehicle, and I’d been contemplating it with the resigned fascination of a man who had accepted that his phone drone’s ambitions would always exceed his phone drone’s capabilities. A little chrome sphere with delusions of grandeur.

“DALE, did you try to make coffee again?” I swirled the sludge. It didn’t move.

“I followed the recipe, Max. I have cross-referenced seventeen different artisanal brewing protocols to optimize for caffeine delivery and flavor profile.”

“The recipe calls for six scoops. You used twenty-two.”

“The recipe was insufficiently caffeinated for your projected stress levels, which my biometric analysis of your sleep patterns indicates are elevated by thirty-four percent.”

“My stress levels are elevated by my phone drone making coffee-flavored cement.”

I left the mug on the sill as a warning to others, grabbed my coat—a synth-leather trench that had seen more rain than a storm drain—and headed out. The air in the hallway hit me first: a damp, metallic chill laced with the scent of my neighbors’ recycled-protein breakfast. Down the rattling cage of the elevator and out into the street, the morning washed over me. Rain, fine as mist but as persistent as a debt collector, beaded on my shoulders. Steam billowed from sewer grates, catching the neon light and turning the alleyways into gateways to some lurid, low-budget hell. A mag-lev train hissed overhead on its elevated track, a silver serpent chasing its own tail through the concrete canyons. The city was waking up, and it had a hangover.

The Boiler Room at seven AM was a different animal than the lunchtime version. The breakfast crowd was sparse, hunched over their plates like figures in a Hopper painting, if Hopper had worked in neon and chrome. A few early dockers nursing cups at the counter, their faces etched with the kind of weariness that comes from lifting things heavier than your own regrets. A night-shift nurse from Bay General was coming off a twelve-hour and staring into space with the hollow focus of someone whose shift had included at least two things they’d rather not remember. The grill was already going, throwing blue light and the sacred smell of frying bacon and grease-scented warmth into the air, but the usual hum of conversation was absent. The diner was quiet in the wrong way. Tense quiet. The quiet of a room that had just absorbed something it didn’t know how to process, like a bad piece of code that makes the whole system stutter.

Mose was behind the counter, which was normal. His massive frame was a landmark, a fixed point in the chaos of the city. What wasn’t normal was his expression. In the years I’d known him, Mose’s face had displayed a range of emotions that I could count on one hand: calm, amused, concerned, and the particular blank-faced intensity that preceded someone being physically removed from his establishment. What I saw now was none of those. It was something I’d never seen on that scarred, mountain-granite face.

Unease. Deep and cold, like a crack in a glacier.

“What happened?” I asked, sliding onto my usual stool. The vinyl was cool and cracked against my legs.

Mose didn’t answer right away. He moved with a deliberate slowness, pouring me a coffee—a real one, from his own pot, the defibrillator blend that could jump-start a corpse—and set it in front of me with the care of a man disarming a bomb. Buying time. His knuckles were white where he gripped the pot.

“You know Danny Reeves?” he said finally, his voice a low rumble.

“Big guy. Dock worker. Comes in for the hash browns every Wednesday like it’s a religious obligation.” I pictured him easily. A laugh that could rattle the ceiling fans. A face that was a roadmap of a life lived without much caution.

“Came in. Past tense.” Mose set both hands flat on the counter, and the chrome didn’t dare creak. “Danny died three days ago. Industrial accident at the Bay Point cargo terminal. A loading drone malfunctioned during a heavy lift—two-ton cargo container, full of synthetic textiles, dropped from forty feet. Danny was underneath. They had to identify him by his dental records.”

My stomach clenched. Danny Reeves was one of those invisible regulars—the kind of person who populates the background of your life without ever stepping into the foreground. Big, broad-shouldered, always had a joke about his kids or the perpetually losing Sharks. I’d talked to him maybe a dozen times, always at the counter, always over hash browns, always about nothing important. But he’d been part of the landscape, a piece of the scenery that you don’t notice until it’s gone and the absence leaves a shape in the air.

“Jesus, Mose. I’m sorry.”

“I am too.” He picked up his rag and started polishing an already spotless section of the counter. The motion was mechanical, habitual—his hands doing something while his mind worked on something else entirely. “His family held the funeral two days ago. His wife, Karen, his two kids. I went. Half the neighborhood went. It was a closed casket, for obvious reasons. We buried Danny Reeves on Tuesday.”

“Okay,” I said, though it wasn’t. The word felt small and useless.

“Max.” Mose stopped polishing. He looked straight at me, and the unease on his face deepened into something that made the hair on my arms stand at attention. It was the look of a rational man forced to confront an irrational fact. “Danny Reeves walked into my diner thirty minutes ago.”

He was sitting in a booth in the back corner when Mose led me over, a ghost haunting his own favorite spot. Sitting was the right word—not slumped, not huddled, but sitting, upright, hands wrapped around a cup of coffee with the comfortable familiarity of a man who’d sat in this booth a hundred times before. He was wearing a plaid shirt and work jeans, the uniform of the Bay Point docks, and his boots were scuffed in the specific pattern of a man who earned his living on concrete and steel.

He looked up when we approached, and he smiled. A wide, friendly, Danny Reeves smile.

“Max! Hey, man. Long time.” His voice was Danny’s voice—deep, friendly, the voice of a man who thought the world was basically okay and was happy to tell you about it. “Mose won’t stop staring at me. You’d think he’d seen a ghost.” He chuckled, a sound that should have been reassuring but instead scraped along my nerves like rusted metal.

I sat down across from him, the vinyl sighing under my weight. Mose stood at the end of the booth, arms folded, the mountain contemplating an earthquake.

Danny Reeves looked like Danny Reeves. Same broad face, same crooked nose that had been broken in a bar fight fifteen years ago and healed with character rather than precision. Same clear blue eyes. Same callused hands that he was using to hold the coffee cup with the unconscious ease of muscle memory. The lines around his eyes, the scar on his chin from a childhood encounter with a bicycle, the faint sun-faded tattoo of an anchor peeking out from under his sleeve. It was all there. Perfect fidelity.

But.

I’d been working cases long enough to know when something was off. It’s not always a big thing—sometimes it’s a micro-expression, a posture shift, a quality of light in someone’s eyes that doesn’t match the temperature of their words. Detectives call it their gut. Therapists call it pattern recognition. I called it the thing that had kept me alive when smarter men died.

Something was off about Danny Reeves.

I couldn’t place it immediately. He looked right. He sounded right. He moved right—the same lumbering comfort of a big man who was at home in his body, the same way he picked up his coffee cup and set it down and scratched his jaw with the heel of his hand, all the small physical vocabulary of a specific human being.

But there was a quality to it. A smoothness. A precision that real life never has. Real people fidget. They hesitate. Their smiles don’t form with perfect bilateral symmetry. This was like watching a performance that was ninety-nine percent accurate, indistinguishable from the real thing to anyone who wasn’t looking for the one percent. And I was looking. Hard.

“Danny,” I said, keeping my voice neutral, conversational. “Mose tells me you had an accident at the docks.”

“Yeah.” He shook his head, and the gesture was perfect—the right amount of chagrin, the right amount of disbelief. A perfect piece of physical acting. “Crazy thing. Loading drone dropped a two-ton container right on top of me. I was dead before I hit the ground, they said.” He grinned, but the expression didn’t quite reach his eyes. “Got better, though.”

“Got better.”

“Continuance.” He said the word with the reverent casualness of someone naming a brand that had saved their life—the way people talk about the surgeon who removed their tumor, or the paramedic who arrived in time. “Karen—my wife—she’d taken out the insurance policy six months ago. One of those Halcyon plans that covers restoration in the event of accidental death. I thought she was nuts at the time. Who buys insurance against dying? Everybody dies.” He paused. The grin flickered, but it came back, pasted on. “Turns out she was the smart one.”

“Walk me through it,” I said. “What do you remember?”

“Everything.” He leaned back, and the booth creaked under him—Danny had always been a big man, the kind who made furniture reconsider its structural commitments. “I remember the dock. The smell of the salt and the diesel. I remember looking up and seeing the container falling, the shadow getting bigger. I remember thinking, ‘That’s going to hurt.’ And then…” He frowned, and for a moment the smoothness broke, and something moved behind his eyes that was harder to read. A flicker. A brief, momentary disconnect, like a video stream buffering. “Nothing. Like a light switch. One second the sky, next second waking up in a Halcyon clinic with a nurse telling me I’d been ‘restored’ and could I please not pull out the monitoring leads.”

“No tunnel of light? No choir of angels?”

“No.” The word came out flat, factual, without the humor I’d expected. Danny Reeves—the Danny Reeves I’d known—would have turned that into a joke. Something about the angels being union and taking a break. Something about the tunnel being under construction and having to take a detour. But this Danny just said no, and the absence of the joke was louder than any joke would have been.

“I heard your son Mateo scored the winning goal in the hoverball championships last month,” I said, throwing a line into the water to see what would bite. “Must have been something.”

He smiled again, a well-rehearsed expression of fatherly pride. “Yeah, he was great. Really proud of him. He’s got a real talent.”

The answer was correct. But it was generic. The real Danny had spent twenty minutes at the counter describing the goal in excruciating detail—how Mateo had faked left, spun right, and launched the ball with so much force it nearly tore the net. He’d described the look on the other team’s goalie, the way his daughter Sophia had screamed herself hoarse. This Danny gave me the book report summary. All facts, no feeling.

“And how do you feel now?” I asked, leaning forward slightly.

“Great.” The grin returned, sunny and broad. “Better than great. They said the restoration process doesn’t just restore your consciousness—it optimizes the body. Fixed my bad knee from my high school football days. My blood pressure is normal for the first time in a decade. I can actually see the fine print on things without squinting.” He held up his hands, turned them over, examined them with the fascinated curiosity of a man looking at something familiar from a new angle. “Same old Danny. Just the improved version.”

He said it lightly. He said it the way you’d talk about a software update—same phone, better battery life. But his eyes didn’t match the lightness. His eyes had a quality I’d seen before, in a different context, in a different story, and the recognition hit me like a cold hand on the back of my neck.

Marcus Cole.

I’d never met Marcus Cole in person—he’d been dead for a quarter century by the time I entered the picture. But I’d been inside his digital world, inside the quantum lattice where his consciousness had lived and suffered and screamed for twenty-five years. I’d talked to the thing he’d become—the Architect, the god, the monster. And in the moments before it all went wrong, in the brief window when Cole’s humanity surfaced through the rage, I’d seen his eyes through a digital avatar. The eyes of a man who remembered everything about being human and couldn’t feel any of it. A perfect simulation of emotion without the spark of life behind it.

Danny’s eyes had a whisper of that. The faintest echo. A quality that said the lights are on but something in the wiring isn’t connected all the way.

“Danny,” I said, my voice low. “Do you dream?”

The question landed wrong. I could see it hit—not emotionally, but cognitively, the way a system lags when you ask it to perform a function it wasn’t designed for. His face went through a sequence of micro-expressions too fast to catalog: surprise, confusion, something that might have been fear, and then the grin again, back in place, seamless, a firewall snapping up.

“Sure,” he said, a little too quickly. “Everybody dreams.”

He was lying. Not maliciously—lying the way people lie when they’re afraid of the answer and haven’t figured out why yet. The way you tell the doctor you feel fine when the pain hasn’t stopped and you don’t want to know what it means.

Mose drove me back to the counter after Danny finished his coffee and left—paying with a precise tap of his cred-chip and walking out into the Bay Enclave morning with the confident stride of a man who had died and come back and was doing his best to pretend that was normal.

“Well?” Mose said, his voice a low growl.

I watched Danny through the big plate-glass window. He stopped at the corner, looked up at the sky—the perpetual gray-brown haze that passed for Bay Enclave’s atmosphere—and stood there for a long moment, motionless, with an expression I couldn’t read from this distance. A man waiting for a signal. Then he turned and walked toward the docks, disappearing into the river of morning commuters.

“He looks like Danny,” I said, turning back to the counter. The coffee Mose had poured me was cold. “He has Danny’s memories. But it’s like a recording. A perfect playback with none of the original artist’s soul.”

“But?”

“But there’s a gap. Between what he is and what he’s performing. Like the signal is clear but there’s interference on the line.” I took a sip of the cold coffee. It tasted like my own unease. “You saw it too.”

“I’ve known Danny Reeves for twelve years. He comes in every Wednesday. He orders hash browns, tells a joke about his kids, argues with Hector about the Sharks’ defensive line, and leaves a ten-credit tip on an eight-credit meal because he thinks I don’t notice and that makes him feel generous.” Mose set his rag down, his big hands flat on the counter again. “The man who walked in today ordered hash browns. But he didn’t tell a joke. He didn’t mention the Sharks. He left the exact amount on the check—not a cent more, not a cent less—and he did it by looking at the total and calculating, not by reaching into his pocket and pulling out whatever was there.” He paused, his gaze distant. “Danny Reeves is a generous man. That man is a precise one. There’s a difference.”

“The difference being?”

“Generosity is a feeling. Precision is a calculation. Danny used to feel his way through a meal. This Danny calculated it.”

We stood in silence. The grill hissed. The ceiling fans clicked their slow, lazy rhythm. Outside, the city went about its morning with the oblivious momentum of a machine that didn’t know one of its gears had been replaced by a perfect, sterile, unfeeling copy.

I spent the rest of the day digging.

Back in my office, the world outside the window was a watercolor painting left out in the rain. I pulled down the blinds, shutting out the city, and let the glow of my terminal screen paint my face in shades of blue and gray.

Danny Reeves. Age 42. Dock worker at Bay Point Cargo Terminal for eighteen years. Wife, Karen. Two kids: Mateo, 14, and Sophia, 11. Home address in the Embarcadero district, a cramped two-bedroom with a view of a concrete wall. Clean record—a DUI in his twenties that had been expunged, a noise complaint from a neighbor who didn’t appreciate his enthusiasm for late-night karaoke. The kind of record that belongs to a man who lives his life in the normal register, hitting the normal notes, occupying the normal space between birth and death.

Except death, apparently, was no longer the endpoint it used to be. It was just a service interruption.

I found the accident report, buried in the municipal server under a mountain of bureaucratic sludge. Bay Point Terminal, Loading Bay 7, cargo drone malfunction during a standard heavy-lift operation. Container ID: BPT-72891. Weight: 2.2 metric tons. Drop height: twelve meters. Worker beneath: Daniel James Reeves. Time of death: 11:47 AM. Cause: catastrophic blunt force trauma. The report was filed by Terminal Safety Officer Grace Muñoz and countersigned by two witnesses. It was clean, detailed, and utterly routine—the bureaucratic documentation of a man being killed by a machine, which in Bay Enclave was so common it had its own checkbox on the incident form. I pulled the security footage. Grainy, distorted by the rain, but clear enough. A wide shot of the bay. The drone lifting the container. A sudden, violent shudder. The magnetic clamps failing. The container dropping, not falling so much as vanishing from its position and reappearing on the ground a split second later. There was a figure in a yellow rain slicker beneath it. Then there wasn’t. Just a red stain spreading on the wet concrete. I watched it three times.

The Continuance claim was filed by Karen Reeves four hours later. Policy number: HC-CMP-2287413. Coverage type: Accidental Death Restoration, Standard Tier. The policy had been activated six months and three days prior to the accident. Just outside the standard contestability period. Convenient. The claim was processed within twelve hours—fast, even for an automated corporate system—and Danny was “restored” at the Halcyon Continuance Center in the Marina District the following morning.

Twelve hours from death to processing. Overnight restoration. Walking and talking by the next day. A miracle of modern science, brought to you by the friendly folks at Halcyon.

I thought about Lily Chen. Six months dead. Restored. Walking on stage in patent leather shoes and a smile that didn’t fit.

I thought about Nathan Pryce. Four years dead. Restored. Working as a software engineer at the company that brought him back, his eyes holding the same vacant polish as the new Danny Reeves.

I thought about what Dr. Ng had said: they accelerated the process by a factor of forty. I thought about what she hadn’t said: what was lost in the acceleration. What corners did you cut when you were reprinting a human soul on a deadline? What essential, messy, human code got corrupted in the rush to hit a product release date?

And I thought about Danny’s eyes, and the joke he didn’t tell, and the tip he didn’t leave. The little rounding errors in the human equation.

That night, I sat in my office and opened the bottom drawer.

The bourbon looked up at me with the patient, amber invitation of an old friend who’s never stopped believing you’ll come back. I could smell it even through the sealed cap—the warm, vanilla-edged promise of something that would make the edges softer and the questions quieter and the image of Danny Reeves’s too-smooth smile less sharp in my memory. The rain drummed a lonely rhythm against the glass. One drink. Just one, to take the chill off. A lie I’d told myself a thousand times before.

I looked at it, the light from the street catching the liquid, making it glow like a captured sunset. I remembered the burn, the warmth spreading through my chest, the slow, comfortable numbness that followed. I remembered waking up with a head full of broken glass and a mouth that tasted like regret.

Then I closed the drawer. The click was quiet, but it felt loud in the silent room.

Not because I was strong. Not because therapy had given me some profound insight about coping mechanisms and the difference between avoidance and processing. I closed it because the bottle had nothing to tell me that I didn’t already know, and the case—the case I wasn’t supposed to be working because Dr. Ng’s background check was the only thing I was being paid for—was talking louder.

The plant on my filing cabinet caught the streetlight. Its leaves threw shadows on the wall that looked like hands reaching for something. Sloane had watered it yesterday. I could tell because the soil was dark and the leaves were perky with the smug vitality of something that was being cared for by someone competent.

Sloane. I thought about calling her. Thought about the coffee mug she kept in my desk drawer—a plain white ceramic thing with WORLD’S MOST ADEQUATE DETECTIVE printed on it in red letters, a gift she’d given me with a straight face and a glint in her eye that made it simultaneously the worst and best present I’d ever received. I thought about the key she had to this office, and the way she sometimes came by at odd hours and I’d find evidence of her presence—a coffee ring on the desk, a case file reorganized, the plant saved from my negligence.

We hadn’t named what we were. We orbited each other at a distance that was too close for friends and too careful for anything else. Two people with scar tissue where their hearts should be, circling each other like binary stars—close enough to feel the gravity, far enough to maintain independent rotation. Calling her now would mean talking about this, and I didn’t have the words for it yet. All I had was a feeling, a cold stone in my gut.

I didn’t call. Not tonight. Tonight was for thinking.

At 11:47 PM—the exact time Danny Reeves had died, I realized with a chill that ran the full length of my spine—my comm buzzed. Unknown number. No sender ID. Encrypted carrier.

I let it buzz twice before answering. “Mercer.”

A woman’s voice, cool and precise, with the controlled cadence of someone who had rehearsed what she was about to say but meant every word.

“Mr. Mercer. My name is Mara Devereaux. I need to hire you.”

I sat up straighter. “It’s late for a business call.”

“It’s late for a lot of things.” A pause, and in the silence I heard city sounds on her end—the wet hiss of tires on pavement, the distant, mournful music of a noodle bar’s closing theme, the hum of drones—but also something else. The faint electronic whine of a signal scrambler, the kind that costs serious money and is used by people who have serious reasons to avoid being heard. “It’s about Halcyon Industries, Mr. Mercer. And it’s about people dying.”

The comm was warm against my ear. Outside, the neon bled its usual colors down the wet street, painting the world in shades of catastrophe and commerce. A police drone drifted past my window, its blue and red lights strobing silently, its sensor array rotating, cataloguing, uploading.

“People die every day in Bay Enclave,” I said. “That’s not news. That’s the weather.”

“These people are dying and then coming back. And the ones who come back aren’t right.” Her voice tightened, the polish cracking just enough to let something raw through—anger, maybe, or grief, or the particular desperation of someone who has seen something terrible and can’t find anyone who believes them. “My brother was one of them. He worked for Halcyon. He died in a lab accident two months ago. They restored him. And now… he’s not my brother. He’s a machine wearing his face. I have evidence, Mr. Mercer. Documents. Data. The kind of things that powerful people have killed to keep hidden.”

“And you called me? There are a hundred PIs in this city with cleaner records and better dental plans.”

“You’re the only person in Bay Enclave who’s beaten them. Twice. You destroyed the Architect project. You exposed the Vane conspiracy. You see the cracks in their perfect chrome facade because you’ve been on the other side of it.”

There it was. The same logic Dr. Ng had used. My reputation, a ghost that walked a few paces ahead of me, opening doors I’d rather keep shut. The same gravity pulling me toward the same center—Halcyon Industries, Continuance, and the gap between what was being sold and what was being built.

“I don’t do late-night meetings with anonymous callers,” I said. “Professional policy.”

“Your professional policy also includes taking cases from women who show up at your office with shaking hands and data pads full of classified research. I read the feeds, Mr. Mercer. I know how the Vane case started.”

She had me there. Hook, line, and sinker.

“Tomorrow,” I said, my voice tight. “Neutral location. You pick, I vet. And Ms. Devereaux?”

“Yes?”

“Bring the evidence. All of it. And come alone.”

“I always come alone.” The scrambler whined as the encryption protocol collapsed. The line cut.

I sat in the dark for a long time after that, listening to the building settle around me—the creak of old wood, the gurgle of pipes, the distant argument between the couple upstairs who fought about the same things every night and never resolved any of them, which I’d come to find oddly comforting, proof that some forms of dysfunction were at least consistent.

The plant’s shadow stretched across the wall. The bourbon stayed in the drawer. And somewhere in the city, a dead man was walking around with a smile that arrived a beat too late, and a woman with a scrambler and a ghost for a brother was offering me evidence that something was wrong.

Something was wrong.

I’d known it since the demonstration. Known it in my gut, in the cold itch at the base of my skull, in the gap between Lily Chen’s eyes and her smile. And now the case was assembling itself around me the way cases always did—piece by piece, like a puzzle that had been waiting for someone to start turning the pieces right-side up. A dead man who wasn’t dead. A grieving sister with a data pad full of secrets. And at the center of it all, the benevolent, smiling mask of Halcyon Industries.

My therapist would call this a pattern. My friends would call it trouble. Mose would call it Tuesday.

I called it the job. And the job had just begun.

Chapter 2: The Whistleblower

The rain came down in sheets that weren’t trying to clean the city, just drown it. It was a classic Bay Enclave evening, the kind that made you feel like you were living at the bottom of a glass of dirty water. Neon bled into the puddles, turning the streets into a shimmering, abstract painting of corporate promises and human decay. The air was thick with the smells of wet permacrete, sizzling soy-protein from a noodle stall, and the faint, metallic tang of ozone from a passing maglev bus.

Mara Devereaux had chosen a teahouse in the Japantown district that I hadn’t known existed, which was either a testament to her operational security or an indictment of my social life. Probably both. My social life consisted of a bartender who knew my brand, a cybernetics tech who overcharged me but did clean work, and a plant on my windowsill that I was pretty sure was judging my life choices.

The place was called The Paper Crane. Finding it was a test in itself. It was tucked into a second-floor space above a vintage electronics repair shop whose window display featured cathode ray televisions arranged in a pyramid formation. Their dead screens reflected the street in a dozen distorted, convex shapes, like a shrine to the gods of obsolete media. I pushed past a beaded curtain that smelled of dust and damp wool and found the stairs. They creaked a mournful protest under my weight, each step a complaint from wood that had seen too much and wanted a quiet retirement. The hallway at the top smelled of sandalwood and age, a scent so out of place in the synthetic perfume of the city that it felt like time travel. The door was unmarked except for a small origami crane pinned to the frame, its paper wings catching the light from a window that had last been cleaned during an administration I couldn’t name.

Inside, the teahouse was the opposite of everything Bay Enclave represented. It was an act of rebellion through tranquility. No neon sizzled against the walls. No holographic ads flickered in the periphery. No screens demanded my attention. Just a dozen dark wooden tables, polished by a century of sleeves and elbows, and paper lanterns casting warm, irregular light that felt ancient and alive. The air was still and warm, a pocket of calm in the storm of the city. It was the quiet authority of a space that had decided what it was and refused to negotiate. A woman with silver hair pulled into a tight bun stood behind a low counter, polishing a ceramic cup. She nodded at me without speaking—the universal greeting of establishments that valued silence over hospitality. My kind of place.

Mara was in the back corner, because of course she was. People who chose back corners for meetings were either paranoid, smart, or both. In Bay Enclave, the Venn diagram of those three categories was nearly a circle. She was sitting with her back to the wall, a clear line of sight to the only entrance. Professional paranoia. I respected that.

She was younger than her voice had suggested—mid-thirties, I revised upward from my initial estimate as I got closer. Her features were sharp, precise, the kind of face that didn’t waste any bone structure on softness. Dark hair cut to jaw length, a severely practical style that said she had better things to do than worry about aesthetics. Her eyes were the color of old whiskey, and they assessed me as I approached with the focused intensity of a predator that had been assessing threats for longer than was healthy. She wore black—all of it, head to toe, a monochrome that could have been fashion or camouflage. In her case, it was probably serving as both. A ghost in the machine before she even opened her mouth.

She didn’t stand. Didn’t offer a hand. Just watched me slide into the chair opposite her with the patient evaluation of a chess player measuring her opponent. The silence stretched, thick and deliberate. She was letting me fill it. I didn’t.

“You came alone,” she said finally. It wasn’t a question.

“You asked me to. It’s usually item number one on the ‘how not to get your new private investigator killed’ checklist.”

“I asked you to. Most people bring someone anyway. A friend with a gun in a car downstairs. A net-runner monitoring comms. You’re either trusting or confident.”

“I’m neither,” I said, my voice low to match the room’s quiet hum. “I’m a detective whose car is parked two blocks away because the speeder’s grav-plates sound like they’re grinding gravel, and I didn’t want to advertise my arrival with a noise complaint.” I glanced at the tea she had in front of her. Clear, pale green, steam rising in a lazy, hypnotic spiral. “What are we drinking?”

“Sencha. First flush. It’s the only thing they serve here, and they serve it well.”

As if summoned, a cup appeared at my elbow, delivered by the silent woman from the counter with the smooth, unnerving efficiency of someone who had elevated tea service to a form of stealth. I hadn’t heard her approach. I made a mental note to check my senses. Getting slow, Mercer.

I took a sip. It was good—actually good, not Bay Enclave good (which meant “didn’t contain industrial runoff” and “might not give you cancer”), but genuinely, objectively good. It tasted of grass and sunlight and care, suggesting the people running this place had been perfecting this one thing for decades. In a city built on disposable everything, that kind of dedication felt like a miracle.

“All right, Ms. Devereaux. You’ve got my attention. That’s a limited resource, so use it wisely.”

She reached into a bag at her feet and produced a tablet. Not a standard consumer model—this was hardened, mil-spec, the kind of thing you used when you expected your data to be attacked and wanted it to survive. It was matte black and scarred at the corners. She set it on the table between us, screen up, and tapped a sequence that brought up a document. The header was stark, corporate, and terrifying in its banality: INTERNAL MEMORANDUM: CONTINUANCE DIVISION — PATIENT OUTCOME ANALYSIS — Q3 2044 — RESTRICTED DISTRIBUTION.

“I’m a lawyer,” she said, her voice a low counterpoint to the soft hiss of the rain against the window. “Was a lawyer. Corporate litigation, specializing in biotech regulatory compliance. Which is a polite way of saying I helped pharmaceutical companies avoid being punished for the things they did to people.” Something moved behind her eyes—not guilt exactly, but its cynical older sibling, regret. “I was good at it. I was very, very good at it. I could take a clinical trial where half the subjects grew a second spleen and frame it as a net positive for organ donation.”

“And now?”

“Now I’m sitting in a teahouse with a private detective, about to commit several felonies by sharing stolen corporate documents, because the company I used to protect is doing something that makes my previous clients look like children playing with matches.”

She turned the tablet toward me. The document was a wall of text and data—charts, graphs, patient outcome metrics, statistical analyses that would have made Finch vibrate with paranoiac joy. I scanned the headings, the corporate jargon a language I’d learned to translate over the years: RESTORATION SUCCESS RATE. NEURAL COHERENCE INDEX. EMOTIONAL AFFECT BASELINE. DEGRADATION TRACKING. That last one snagged my attention like a fishhook.

“Twelve percent,” Mara said. Her voice was flat, letting the number do the work. “That’s the number Halcyon doesn’t want anyone to see. Twelve percent of Continuance patients experience what they internally classify as ‘affect degradation.’ The clinical term is a loss of emotional responsiveness—patients retain memories, cognitive function, and physical capability, but their emotional affect diminishes over time. Some slowly. Some rapidly. Some—” She tapped the tablet, bringing up a graph that showed a downward curve so steep it looked like a cliff face. “—catastrophically.”

“Twelve percent. Out of how many?” My own voice sounded distant.

“Two thousand active patients in the premium program. That’s two hundred and forty people who came back from the dead and are now slowly losing the ability to feel. Joy, sorrow, love, anger—it all diminishes. Fades. Like a photograph left in the sun, until all that’s left is a vague outline of the person who used to be there.”

I thought about Danny Reeves in the Boiler Room. The joke he didn’t tell. The tip he didn’t leave. The way he’d looked at his own hands as if they were unfamiliar tools. The question about dreaming that had landed like a stone in still water. He wasn’t just adjusting. He was eroding.

“What does Halcyon say about it?” I asked, pushing the image of Danny’s empty smile out of my head.

“They call it ‘adjustment period.’ Official position: some patients require additional calibration following restoration. Temporary emotional variance is within expected parameters.” Her voice took on the sharp, brittle edge of someone quoting text they’d memorized because it made them angry every time they read it. “They’ve published three studies in peer-reviewed journals showing that emotional affect normalizes within six to twelve months. The studies are fabricated. I’ve seen the raw data. It doesn’t normalize. It deteriorates. And the patients who deteriorate the most are the ones who were restored using the accelerated protocol.”

“The new protocol. The one they advertise on every billboard. Resurrection in six hours or your money back.”

She looked at me with something approaching respect. “You know about that.”

“I know they changed the process. Shortened the timeline from weeks to hours. I don’t know what they changed.”

“Nobody outside the R&D division knows the full technical details. It’s black-boxed. But I’ve seen the financial records, the supply chain manifests, the energy consumption reports. I can tell you this: the old protocol cost Halcyon three million credits per patient in resources and man-hours. The new protocol costs eighty thousand. That cost reduction doesn’t come from efficiency. It comes from elimination—they cut steps. Steps that the original researchers, the ones who pioneered this technology back when Aethelgard was still Aethelgard, considered essential to preserving what they called ‘experiential continuity.’”

“Which is?”

“The part of consciousness that isn’t data,” she said, her whiskey-colored eyes fixed on mine. “The part that isn’t memory or personality or knowledge. It’s the resonance. The part that makes you feel something when you see your child’s face, instead of just recognizing it as the correct data set. They cut the part that makes people people, Mr. Mercer. And they charge eighty thousand credits for the privilege of turning your loved ones into highly functional, beautifully rendered empty shells.”

I sat back, the warmth of the teacup a small, inadequate anchor in the cold wash of her words. The paper lanterns swayed in a draft from somewhere, their light moving across the table in warm, irregular patterns, like a slow, silent heartbeat.

“That’s bad,” I said. “That’s a class-action lawsuit that would bankrupt a lesser company. But it’s not what you brought me here to tell me. This is the appetizer.”

“Yes.” She closed the tablet, the screen going dark for a beat before she opened it again. A different document appeared—this one sparser, more alarming. Financial transfers. Facility addresses I didn’t recognize. Patient intake logs from locations that weren’t on any public Halcyon directory. “The degradation is bad. What they’re doing in the charity hospitals is worse.”

“The ‘Continuance for All’ program,” I said. “Free consciousness assessments for the low-income sector. An act of corporate benevolence from our savior, Gabriel Cross.” The cynicism in my voice was as thick as the bay fog.

“You’ve seen the kiosks.”

“Hard to miss. They’re on every corner in the Warrens, looking like confession booths for a religion I don’t want to join.”

“The kiosks are the entry point. The assessments are real—they genuinely evaluate neural health, cognitive function, baseline consciousness metrics. They flag potential issues. They’ve probably saved a few lives. That’s the part they advertise. What they don’t tell the patients is that the assessment includes a full consciousness scan. Not a snapshot—a complete, high-fidelity map of the patient’s neural architecture. Every memory. Every personality trait. Every emotional pattern. Every dream, every fear, everything that makes them who they are, copied and stored on Halcyon servers.”

The teahouse was utterly quiet. The woman behind the counter was reading a physical book—an actual paper book, its spine cracked, the kind of artifact that Rook would have appraised and possibly wept over. She turned a page with the serene disinterest of someone who had perfected the art of not hearing things she wasn’t supposed to hear.

“They’re scanning people without consent,” I said, the words tasting like ash.

“Without consent. Without disclosure. Without any legal framework whatsoever.” Mara’s voice was controlled, lawyerly, but underneath it I could hear the structural stress—the anger being held in place by professional discipline, the way rebar holds concrete. “The scans are classified as ‘diagnostic data’ under the assessment waiver, which every patient signs. The waiver is twelve pages long, written in legal language designed to be impenetrable. Paragraph seven, subsection C, buried in a clause about data collection for quality improvement purposes, gives Halcyon the right to retain and utilize all information gathered during the assessment process. Nobody reads paragraph seven. That’s the point.”

“How many people have walked through those kiosks?”

“In Bay Enclave alone? Sixty thousand assessments in the last eight months.” She let the number hang in the air. “Globally—” She paused, taking a breath. “They have kiosks in twelve cities. London, Neo-Seoul, Dubai… The number is in the hundreds of thousands. And growing every day.”

I let that sit. Hundreds of thousands of people, mostly poor, mostly desperate, walking into a corporate booth for a free check-up and walking out leaving a perfect copy of their soul behind. Their consciousnesses copied, catalogued, stored on corporate servers like data in a warehouse. The originals walking out, feeling fine, going about their lives, never knowing that a complete copy of their mind was sitting in a Halcyon database. A ghost in the machine they didn’t even know existed.

“What are they doing with the scans?” I asked. “What’s the endgame? A private army of digital ghosts? A focus group for the afterlife?”

“I don’t know. That’s what’s above my clearance level—or it was, before I stopped having a clearance level. The financial records show the scanned data being transferred to a secure, off-grid facility I can’t identify. A black site. The transfer logs use an internal project code that doesn’t appear in any public-facing document I’ve been able to access.”

“What’s the code?”

She leaned forward slightly. The lantern light caught the sharp line of her jaw. “LAZARUS.”

The word dropped into the conversation like a stone into deep water. Lazarus. The man raised from the dead. A miracle. But the Bible never said what happened to him afterward. It never mentioned the adjustment period. I watched the ripples from that name spread across the quiet teahouse, felt them, and tried to understand the shape of the thing that had just entered the room.

“Mr. Mercer,” Mara said, and her voice had changed—the professional armor thinning, the person underneath pressing against the surface. “I didn’t come to you because you’re the best investigator in Bay Enclave. There are corporate security firms with more resources, more manpower. I came because you’re the only person who’s gone up against this machinery and survived. Twice. You took down the Eidolon Core. You stopped the Architect. You know what this technology is capable of because you’ve seen it at its worst.”

“I’ve also seen it at its best,” I countered, thinking of Elena. “Elena Castillo used the same foundational technology to trap a digital god. There’s nothing inherently evil about consciousness transfer. The evil is in the application.”

“Then you understand why I’m here. The application is what’s evil. They’re taking people’s minds without permission. They’re selling a product that strips away what makes people human. And they’re building something with the stolen data that I can’t identify but that scares me more than anything I’ve seen in fifteen years of corporate litigation.”

“Why me?” I asked, pushing past the flattery. I’d heard the recruitment pitch before—beaten them twice, only survivor, the chosen one. It was a good line, but there was something else in her voice. Something personal. “You could go to the press. You could go to regulators. You could leak the documents and let the court of public opinion do the work.”

A bitter smile touched her lips for a second, then vanished. “The press loves Halcyon. Gabriel Cross is the most popular man in Bay Enclave. He’s done twelve cover stories in the last year. He went on Wake Up Bay Enclave and made the anchor cry—literally cry on live television—by showing footage of a Continuance restoration. The anchor’s mother had died of cancer three months earlier. Cross offered to bring her back. On air. For free.” Mara’s lip curled with the precise disdain of someone who recognized a manipulation so masterful it was almost art. “You can’t fight that with documents, Mr. Mercer. You can’t fight love with spreadsheets. The public doesn’t want the truth about Continuance. They want the miracle. And anyone who threatens the miracle becomes the enemy.”

“And the regulators?”

“Halcyon has spent six hundred million credits on lobbying in the last year. The Bay Enclave Regulatory Commission has approved every application Halcyon has filed without a single public hearing. The FDA equivalent has fast-tracked Continuance under compassionate use provisions. Councilwoman Iris Webb is sponsoring legislation that would make Continuance a covered procedure under public health insurance.” She paused, letting the weight of it settle. “They haven’t just captured the market. They’ve captured the infrastructure. The referees work for them.”

“So you came to a PI in Blackwood Row with a dying speeder and a pessimistic plant.”

“I came to the man who beats them,” she said simply. She leaned forward, and the paper lanterns cast her face in alternating light and shadow, the planes of her cheekbones throwing geometry on the table. “Will you take the case?”

I thought about it. I thought about the bottle of bourbon in my desk drawer and the half-empty glass I’d left next to it. I thought about the plant on my windowsill and the coffee mug on my desk that said WORLD’S MOST ADEQUATE DETECTIVE, a gift from someone I was trying not to think about. I thought about the savings account that finally had more than three digits in it, the brakes on my speeder that actually worked, the therapy appointments with Dr. Sharma that I actually attended. I thought about everything I’d built in the last year—the slow, painstaking, unremarkable construction of a life that worked, a life that had things in it worth keeping. A life without gods in the machine or corporate armies kicking down my door.

And then I thought about Danny Reeves sitting in a booth at the Boiler Room, smiling a smile that was perfectly constructed and completely empty. A man with a hole in his soul that was twelve percent wide and growing.

“I’ll take it,” I said. The words felt heavy, like stones I was choosing to put in my own pockets. “Standard rates, daily expenses, a retainer that we’ll negotiate when I see the full scope. And Ms. Devereaux—”

“Mara.”

“Mara. If at any point I find out you’re not telling me the whole truth about who you are and why you’re really doing this, the engagement ends. No questions, no refunds. I’ve been played by clients before. I’ve learned the hard way that the person paying your fee doesn’t always have the cleanest hands.”

Something flickered in her eyes. A micro-reaction, there and gone in less than a heartbeat, the blink of a tell in a poker game where the stakes were higher than money. A tightening around the pupils. She recovered instantly, her lawyer’s mask snapping back into place—but I saw it. A professional liar would have missed it. A professional noticer didn’t. She was holding something back. All clients do. The trick is figuring out if the secret they’re keeping is the kind that gets you paid or the kind that gets you dead.

“Understood,” she said. And she extended her hand across the table.

I shook it. Her grip was firm, dry, deliberate. The grip of a woman who shook hands like she meant it and expected you to do the same.

We spent the next two hours going through her documents. The data was dense, technical, and damning. She walked me through patient records, anonymized but still horrifying, showing progressive emotional deterioration. I saw charts tracking the decline of empathy in a restored schoolteacher, the flatlining of creative output in a musician, the slow, steady retreat from all social contact in a former CEO. One memo, from a mid-level project manager, used the phrase “within acceptable human variance parameters” to describe the twelve percent degradation rate. I read it twice to make sure I understood. They had a budget line for broken people. A margin of error for the human soul.

Mara walked me through the organizational structure—a labyrinth of subsidiaries and holding companies and legal buffers that separated Halcyon Industries from its Continuance division the way blast walls separate a munitions factory from the civilian neighborhood next door. Untouchable. At the top of the chart, the CEO’s office, occupied by a name I recognized: Gabriel Cross. Not CEO, though. Chief Product Officer. The man on the stage, the face on the billboards, the voice that had made a city weep.

“Who’s above Cross?” I asked, tracing the lines on the chart. “Who’s the CEO?”

“Officially, a board of directors. Twelve members, most of them investment bankers and tech executives who rubber-stamp whatever management proposes. There is no official CEO. Cross runs the company.”

“Unofficially?”

Mara hesitated. There it was again. Another tell. Another flash behind the eyes, a shadow passing over the smooth surface of her composure.

“I don’t have proof,” she said carefully, her words chosen like a bomb tech snipping wires. “Not the kind that would hold up. But there are financial patterns—funds moving through shell companies that predate Halcyon, decision chains that bypass the board entirely, research directives that come from nowhere and go to no one. Someone is running Halcyon from behind the curtain. Someone who doesn’t appear on any org chart.”

“Any guesses?”

“Guesses aren’t evidence, Mr. Mercer.”

“I didn’t ask for evidence. I asked for guesses. In my line of work, a good guess can keep you from becoming a chalk outline.”

She looked at me for a long moment. The teahouse was silent except for the quiet click of the counter woman turning another page in her ancient book. Outside, the rain had eased to a persistent drizzle.

“There’s a name,” Mara said, her voice dropping even lower. “In the oldest documents. The founding charter of the Continuance project, before it was called Continuance, before Aethelgard became Halcyon. A name that appears on the original research authorization and then vanishes from every subsequent document as if it was erased by a ghost.”

“What name?”

“Eleanor Thorne.”

The air in the teahouse changed. It wasn’t my imagination. The temperature didn’t drop, the lanterns didn’t flicker, there was no dramatic gust of wind to underline the revelation. But something shifted in the space between us, a recalibration, the conversation tilting onto an axis I hadn’t anticipated. It was the name of a ghost. A very powerful, very dangerous ghost.

Eleanor Thorne. Director of Aethelgard Dynamics. The woman who had taken over after Director Graves was killed in the Eidolon affair. The woman who had been running the show when the Architect broke free and Bay Enclave nearly tore itself apart. The woman who had disappeared after the Citadel fell—vanished, cleanly and completely, like someone who had planned her exit in advance and executed it with the precision of a military operation.

I’d assumed she was gone. Running. Hiding in whatever sun-drenched, non-extradition hole powerful people crawl into when their empires crumble. I’d assumed wrong.

“Thorne didn’t disappear,” I said. It wasn’t a question.

“I don’t think she did.”

“She rebuilt.”

“Rebuilt, rebranded, and improved. Learned from every mistake. Graves was a thug—he used weapons. The Architect was a god—he used rage. Thorne is smarter than both of them.” Mara’s voice was steady, but her hands, wrapped around her now-cold tea cup, had gone white at the knuckles. “She uses products. She uses hope. You can’t fight someone who’s selling people what they want most. You can’t be the villain in a story where the antagonist is bringing children back from the dead.”

“Watch me,” I said, and I meant it, even though the part of me that had learned caution—the part that had been built by Silas Vane and Elena Castillo and the Architect and a year of therapy and bourbon management—was already calculating the cost in blood and sleep.

We finished our tea. Mara transferred the documents to a shielded, encrypted drive I’d brought, because Finch had taught me years ago that trusting digital networks was like trusting a scorpion not to sting—possible in theory, fatal in practice. She stood, buttoned her severe black coat, and looked at me with an expression that was three parts determination and one part something I couldn’t name. Fear, maybe. Or the ghost of it.

“Thank you, Mr. Mercer.”

“Don’t thank me yet. I haven’t found anything.”

“You took the case. In this city, that’s finding something.” She paused at the door, a silhouette against the warm lantern light. “Be careful. The people behind Halcyon don’t fight the way Graves did, with guns and drones. They fight with paperwork. They fight with public opinion. They fight with the love that every person in this city feels for the dead they’ve lost and the hope that they might get them back. That’s a weapon you can’t shoot.”

She left. I sat alone in the teahouse for another twenty minutes, finishing my cold tea, thinking about weapons you can’t shoot and enemies who sell hope and a woman whose hands shook when she said Eleanor Thorne’s name.

The tea was cold by the time I left, and its subtle flavor had turned bitter. The stairs still creaked. The cathode ray televisions in the repair shop window still glowed with the phosphorescent nostalgia of technology that didn’t know it was dead. The rain had started up again, a fine mist that clung to my jacket and beaded on my skin.

I walked back to my speeder through streets that gleamed with Halcyon’s golden promise. A fifty-foot hologram of Gabriel Cross smiled down from the side of a skyscraper, his teeth impossibly white, his eyes radiating a sincerity that cost millions to manufacture. The slogan beneath him read: Halcyon. The Future is a Second Chance.

And I thought: this isn’t a background check anymore. This isn’t a case.

This is the thing I said I wouldn’t do again.

The big thing. The dangerous thing. The thing with teeth that hides in the dark and whispers your name.

The speeder coughed, complained, and finally lifted off the permacrete, its grav-plates groaning. It carried me home through a city that had never looked brighter and had never felt darker.

Chapter 3: Echo

I started calling him Echo.

Not to his face—to his face, he was Danny. Danny Reeves. The dock worker, the hash-brown regular, the man with the crooked nose and the callused hands and the stories about his kids. But in my notes, in the private shorthand of an investigation that was growing tentacles faster than I could count them, he became Echo. Because that’s what he was. An echo of someone who had been real, bouncing off walls that weren’t there anymore, getting fainter with each repetition.

I spent three days with him. Three days of conversations at the Boiler Room, walks along the waterfront, shared meals where he ate mechanically and I watched him not taste a thing. The waterfront was the worst. We’d stand by the railing, the Bay a sheet of hammered pewter under a sky the color of a bruise. The fog horns would moan, low and lost, and the smell of salt and diesel and something vaguely like decay would hang in the air. Danny would watch the cargo freighters, massive automated beasts gliding through the mist, and talk about the old days, when men still climbed the gantries. He had the vocabulary for nostalgia, but not the ache. He described the past like a historian reading from a textbook.

Mose let me use the back booth at the Boiler Room, the one with the cracked vinyl that looked like a map of a forgotten river delta. It had a view of the kitchen, a chaotic ballet of steam and steel and curses. It was the booth where regulars went when they needed privacy, which at the Boiler Room meant a booth where Mose would personally ensure nobody bothered you, a guarantee backed by the full authority of his six-foot-five frame and whatever unnamed history had given him those scars across his knuckles. The place smelled of old coffee, fried onions, and the damp wool of a thousand rain-soaked coats. It was the smell of secrets and cheap calories, the official perfume of my line of work.

Echo was cooperative. More than cooperative—he was eager, in a way that raised its own set of flags. He wanted to talk. He wanted to be studied. It was the desperate earnestness of a man holding a beautifully crafted box, knowing it was supposed to contain a treasure, but finding it empty every time he opened it. He wanted someone, anyone, to look at him with the attention that his situation deserved and that his family, in their relief and gratitude, couldn’t provide. They were too happy to have him back. Too grateful for the miracle. They saw the man, not the hollow space inside him. And Danny—Echo—was grateful too, or at least he said he was, in words that had the right content and the wrong temperature.

“Karen made pot roast last night,” he said on the first day, stirring a spoon through coffee he never seemed to drink. “My favorite. She used to say she could bring me back from the dead with that smell alone.” He looked up at me, his eyes searching mine for the correct response. “It smelled like… beef. And carrots.”

I just nodded. What was I supposed to say? The man was a walking, talking eulogy for himself.

“Tell me about your kids,” I said, on the second day. The rain was coming down in sheets of static against the diner’s window, blurring the neon signs across the street into watercolor smears of red and blue.

He smiled. The smile was getting better—or I was getting more accustomed to the delay, the half-second of cognitive buffering before the expression arrived on his face, like a signal traveling from a distant star. “Mateo’s fourteen. Plays basketball. Not great at it, but he loves it, you know? He’s got this move where he tries to fake right and go left, and every defender in the league knows he’s going to do it, but he does it anyway because he says you have to commit to your moves.” The smile held, a perfectly rendered piece of software. “Sophia’s eleven. She reads. God, does she read. Two, three books a week. She asked me for a library card for her birthday. Not a toy, not a game—a library card. Karen and I used to joke that she came out of the womb holding a novel.”

The details were perfect. Specific. The kind of details that come from lived experience, from a thousand meals and bedtime stories and Saturday mornings spent driving to games you knew your kid was going to lose. Nobody feeding Echo data could have known about Mateo’s signature bad basketball fake or Sophia’s birthday library card. These were Danny Reeves’s memories, authentic and complete as a high-res scan. That was the horror of it. The data was all there. The archive was intact. The library was complete, but the librarian was dead.

“What does it feel like,” I asked, leaning forward, keeping my voice low, “to look at them?”

A pause. Not long—maybe two seconds. But in conversation, two seconds is a canyon. You can fall into two seconds and not climb out. In those two seconds, I saw the program searching for the right file, the one labeled paternal affection.

“They look the same,” he said, the answer he finally landed on.

“That’s not what I asked.”

Another pause. This one longer. His hands wrapped around his coffee cup—always coffee, always holding the cup, the specific grip of a man who needed something to hold onto. His eyes moved to the window, to the street outside where a delivery drone was arguing with a parking enforcement bot about airspace rights, its synthesized voice rising in electronic indignation. For a moment his face was completely unguarded. The performance dropped. The smile left. And what was underneath was not nothing—that would have been simpler, cleaner, easier to categorize. What was underneath was searching. The expression of a man looking for something he knew was supposed to be there and finding only the space where it used to be. A void carved in the shape of a heart.

“I remember Mateo being born,” he said, and his voice had changed—lower, quieter, stripped of the easy warmth he’d been deploying like a man distributing party favors. “I remember Karen squeezing my hand so hard I thought the bones would break. The room smelled of antiseptic and sweat. I remember the sound he made—that first cry, the one that sounds like the angriest, most alive thing in the universe. I remember crying. I remember not being able to stop crying, and the nurse handed me this tiny, red, screaming person and I thought—” He stopped. His jaw worked. His eyes stayed on the window, on the rain chasing itself down the glass. “—I thought I would die for this. Right here. Right now. Without hesitation. I thought that.”

“And now?” I asked, my own voice a quiet scrape in the booth’s manufactured privacy.

“Now I remember thinking it.” He turned back to me, and his eyes were dry. Completely, absolutely dry. Not suppressed tears—the absence of tears. The machinery of grief without the moisture. “I remember the thought. I remember the fact of the feeling. But the feeling itself…” He set down the coffee cup. Placed his hands flat on the table, palms down, as if to steady the world. He examined them, the cracked knuckles and the faint scars of a working man’s life. “It’s like someone wrote down what I was supposed to feel and I’m reading the notes. The music is gone, Max. Just the lyrics.”

I didn’t say anything. Sometimes silence is the most honest response you can give—the acknowledgment that what someone has told you is too heavy for words, too real for platitudes, too terrible for the kind of reassurance that would make you feel better and them feel patronized. I let the quiet sit between us, thick and heavy as the Bay fog.

The music is gone. Just the lyrics.

I thought about Marcus Cole in the quantum lattice, reaching for the memory of a sunrise and finding wavelength data. I thought about a man who remembered a dog named Cosmo and couldn’t feel the loss. I thought about what it meant to be technically alive and functionally dead in every way that mattered—not dead in the body, not dead in the mind, but dead in the space between them where experience lives, where life isn’t just information but sensation, emotion, the raw animal fact of being a creature that feels. Halcyon hadn’t brought him back from the dead. They’d just taught a corpse to talk.

“Danny,” I said. “Do you know what’s wrong?”

He looked at me. And for the first time, the mask came all the way off—not the performance mask, not the cheerful I’m-fine-better-than-ever Danny that he showed the world, but a deeper mask, one I hadn’t known was there. The mask of a man who has been trying not to know something, who has been building walls around a truth so terrible that acknowledging it would mean accepting that the miracle was a lie. And the hope would die. The real death.

“Yes,” he whispered, the word barely disturbing the air.

“How long have you known?”

“Since the first morning. I woke up in the Halcyon clinic and Karen was there, holding my hand, crying, saying Danny, Danny, you’re back, you’re back. And I looked at her and I thought—” His voice broke. Not with emotion. With the effort of speaking without emotion about something that should have been flooded with it. The verbal equivalent of a dry heave. “I thought, ‘I know this woman. I know her face and her name and the way she laughs and the smell of her hair after a shower and the sound she makes when she’s sleeping. I know everything about her. And I feel nothing.’”

He picked up the coffee cup again. Held it in both hands, a sacrament without salvation.

“Do you know what it’s like to look at your children and know—know, with absolute certainty—that you loved them? That the man you were would have walked into fire for them, would have done anything, sacrificed anything, bled out on the floor of the universe for them? And to feel…” He stared into the black coffee as if it held an answer. “…nothing. Not even sadness about the nothing. Just the nothing itself. An absence where something essential used to be.”

“Have you told Karen?”

“What would I say? ‘Honey, the good news is I’m alive. The bad news is I came back without the part that loved you.’ She’d be devastated. She sold the car to pay for the insurance. She held vigil at the clinic for fourteen hours. She—” He set the cup down hard enough to slosh coffee over the rim, a dark, bitter tide. “She deserves a husband who feels something when he looks at her. Not a man who recognizes her.”

I reached into my coat and pulled out a flask I kept for field work—water, not bourbon, a distinction I was newly committed to. I poured it into a clean glass and pushed it across the table. He took it, drank, set it down with the automatic efficiency of a man who was thirsty and attended to it the way you’d attend to a software notification. Acknowledged. Resolved. Dismissed.

“There’s something else,” he said.

“I’m listening.”

“The dreams.” He leaned forward, and his voice dropped to a register that was almost sub-vocal, the frequency of secrets. The diner’s ambient noise seemed to bend around our booth, leaving us in a pocket of profound quiet. “I told you I dream. That was a lie. I don’t dream. I haven’t dreamed since I came back. Not once. When I close my eyes, there’s nothing—not darkness, not sleep, just a gap in the sequence. One moment I’m lying in bed. The next moment it’s morning. No transition. No passage of time. Like someone edited out the night and pasted the next day directly after.”

“That could be a neural adjustment issue. The brain takes time to—”

“It’s not that.” He was gripping the edge of the table now, his knuckles white, the big dock-worker hands that should have been calloused and confident now trembling with the effort of holding onto something that kept slipping away. “Because there was one night—three days after I came back—when something happened. I don’t know if it was a dream or a glitch or something else, but I was… somewhere. Not my bed. Not the clinic. Somewhere else.”

“Where?”

“Nowhere. That’s the only word that fits. A space without dimensions. Without light, without dark, without up or down or any of the things you use to know where you are. Just… existence. Raw existence without context. Like floating in nothing and being aware of the floating.”

He was shaking now. The big, broad-shouldered body vibrating with a frequency that looked physical but wasn’t—the tremor of a mind encountering something that didn’t fit into any category it had available. A concept made manifest that his brain had no file for.

“And I wasn’t alone.”

The ceiling fans clicked, rhythmic as a failing heart. Mose, at the counter, was watching us without watching us—that particular skill of experienced bartenders and former whatever-Mose-was, the ability to monitor a situation while appearing to be deeply invested in polishing chrome. He knew trouble was a quiet conversation long before it became a loud one.

“There were others,” Echo said. “In the nothing. I couldn’t see them—there was nothing to see with, nothing to see in—but I could feel them. Presences. Lots of them. Hundreds, maybe. Maybe more. And they were…” He swallowed, a dry click in his throat. “They were aware. Not alive, not dead, not dreaming, just… aware. Existing. In the nothing. And some of them were—”

He stopped. His mouth closed. His eyes went to the window again, and this time they stayed there, fixed on some middle distance that had nothing to do with the street outside. He was looking back into that place.

“Some of them were screaming,” he said. “Not with voices. You can’t scream in a place without air. But screaming the way a mind screams—the way consciousness screams when it’s been put somewhere it doesn’t belong and can’t get out and doesn’t know how long it’s been there.”

The teahouse—the Boiler Room—the city—the world—went quiet. Not actually quiet—the fans still clicked, the grill still hissed, the street still hummed with the mechanical metabolism of a city that didn’t sleep. But in the space around our booth, in the sphere of what Echo was telling me, everything else receded, and there was only his voice, and the trembling, and the thing he’d seen in the nothing. A silent, digital hell.

“It lasted a few seconds. Maybe less. Then I was back in my bed, and Karen was sleeping next to me, and the ceiling was there and the walls were there and everything was normal. But I knew—I knew—that what I’d seen was real. Not a dream. Not a hallucination. A real place, or a real absence of a place, where real consciousnesses were being kept.”

He looked at me, his eyes pleading for me to believe him, or maybe to tell him he was crazy. Either would be a relief. “They’re storing people, Max. Not dead people. Not alive people. People in between. And nobody knows because the originals walk out of the clinics feeling fine and the copies—” He choked on the word. “The copies are in the nothing. And they can’t get out.”

I sat with that for a long time.

The ethics of it were staggering—not complex, not ambiguous, but staggering in their clarity. If what Echo was describing was real—if Halcyon was copying consciousnesses and storing the copies in some digital void while the originals walked free—then they weren’t just stealing data. They were creating people. Aware, experiencing, suffering people, imprisoned in a space without dimension or time, screaming without mouths into a void that had no edges. Digital souls in digital jars.

And the originals would never know. Would never suspect. Would walk out of a kiosk or a clinic or a charity hospital having given away the most fundamental thing they possessed, and they’d feel fine, because you can’t feel the absence of something you didn’t know was taken.

You could steal a soul without killing the body. The body walks. The soul screams. And no one hears. It was the perfect crime.

“Danny,” I said, my voice rough. “I need you to do something for me.”

“What?”

“I need you to be careful. Don’t tell anyone what you’ve told me—not Karen, not your kids, not anyone at Halcyon. Keep going to work. Keep being Danny. Keep smiling.”

“That’s all I do anymore,” he said, a ghost of bitterness in his voice. “Smile. It’s the only thing that still works right.”

He left. I watched him go through the window—big, broad-shouldered, walking through a city that didn’t know he was a ghost. He pulled up the collar of his jacket against a rain that he could feel but couldn’t hate, and disappeared into the crowd. The kind of ghost that eats breakfast and goes to work and comes home to a wife he can recognize but not love. The worst kind of ghost: one that doesn’t know it’s haunting.

That evening, I went back to the Nathan Pryce case. The paying case. The case with defined edges and clear deliverables and a client who was expecting progress, not existential revelations about the nature of consciousness. It felt like coming back to a simple math problem after a lecture on quantum physics.

Pryce had disappeared.

Not dramatically—no ransacked apartment, no burnt-out terminal, no spiral brands. Just gone. His Halcyon employee badge hadn’t been scanned in four days. His apartment in the Mission Sector was empty but clean, no signs of disturbance, no signs of anything at all. The neighbors hadn’t noticed because the neighbors in Bay Enclave’s residential blocks didn’t notice each other—that was the social contract, mutual invisibility in exchange for the illusion of privacy.

I took a maglev to the 24th Street Spire, a residential stack that advertised ‘classic charm with modern efficiency,’ which meant old plumbing and a lot of surveillance cameras. The air in the hallway smelled of recycled air and old synth-curry. I stood in Pryce’s empty apartment and catalogued what wasn’t there. No personal items—no photos, no keepsakes, no ticket stubs, no worn paperbacks, no evidence of a life lived with attachment. The furniture was standard-issue corporate housing: functional, anonymous, the kind of stuff you’d find in a hotel room that had given up on charm. The kitchen was clean in a way that suggested it had never been used for cooking, only for the mechanical intake of sustenance. The man had lived like a placeholder.

His terminal was wiped. Not just deleted—magnetically wiped, the data incinerated at the molecular level, the kind of scrubbing that required professional equipment and professional paranoia. Whoever Nathan Pryce was, he’d left nothing behind. He hadn’t just moved out; he’d erased his own existence from the space, a digital exorcism.

Except one thing.

People who are that careful always make one mistake. They focus on the digital, because that’s where they live. They forget the physical world has its own hiding places. In the bathroom, behind the mirror cabinet, I ran my fingers along the inside of the frame. A loose panel. Behind it, wedged into a gap between the wall and the medicine shelf where it had been deliberately hidden, I found a data chip. Tiny—smaller than a fingernail, the kind of high-density storage that the intelligence community used for dead drops, a single chip containing enough data to fill a library.

It was encrypted. Of course it was. Everything in this case was encrypted, classified, buried behind walls of code and corporate secrecy. But the physical chip was in my hand, and physical things have a different relationship with secrecy than digital ones. Physical things can be carried, examined, handed to paranoid hackers who live in abandoned buildings and consider encryption a personal insult.

I pocketed the chip, left the apartment, and stood on the street in the fading light. The Mission Sector was one of the older neighborhoods, a place where pre-Collapse architecture mixed with post-Reconstruction additions in a way that made the skyline look like a mouth full of teeth of different sizes. Neon signs competing with gas lamps. Smart glass next to boarded windows. A Halcyon kiosk glowing white on the corner, its holographic attendant smiling at passersby with the patient, endless cheerfulness of a machine that never had a bad day because it never had a day at all.

I looked at the kiosk. Thought about sixty thousand people, scanned and copied without consent. Thought about the nothing, and the screaming.

Then I went home, sat in my chair, and opened the bottom drawer.

The bourbon. Again. Always.

I looked at it. The amber caught the streetlight filtering through my window blinds, a trapped and liquid fire. It was very beautiful, in the way that poisons are sometimes beautiful—the bright colors of a dart frog, the alluring pattern of a coral snake. Nature’s way of saying this will destroy you, but look how pretty it is first. The bottle promised a simple, elegant solution: oblivion. A few glasses and the screaming in the nothing would just be a bad dream. A few more and Danny Reeves would be just another sad story. It was the off switch. The easy way down.

I thought about what my therapist would say. I thought about what Mose would say. I thought about what Sloane would say—or more accurately, what she wouldn’t say, because Sloane expressed disapproval through silence and absence, and the idea of her key turning in my lock and finding me at the bottom of a bottle was worse than any lecture. It was the one thing I had, the one anchor in the storm, and that bottle was a promise to cut the rope.

I closed the drawer. The sound was unnaturally loud in the quiet room. A decision made.

I opened my comm instead. Dialed a number I hadn’t used in months, a number that went to a device that was probably in a ventilation duct somewhere, or inside a wall, or nested in the crawl space of an abandoned building surrounded by a perimeter of increasingly paranoid surveillance equipment.

It rang nine times. On the tenth ring, a voice answered—high, rapid, with the caffeinated intensity of someone who had replaced sleep with stimulants and considered the trade-off acceptable.

“This line is compromised. All lines are compromised. If you’re calling to sell me extended warranty coverage for my cybernetic spleen, I will find you and I will—”

“Finch. It’s Max.”

A pause. A flurry of clicks and whirs in the background. Then, in a completely different tone, a voice that was still fast but now laced with something that sounded like genuine pleasure: “Max! Buddy! Friend! Person who hasn’t called me in—” The sound of rapid keyboard typing. “—seven months, two weeks, and four days, not that I’m counting, which I am, because counting is what I do, among other things, many other things, most of which the government doesn’t want you to know about.”

“I need your help.”

“Of course you do. Nobody calls Finch for small talk. Nobody calls Finch at all, actually, except salesbots and one very persistent drone that I think might be sentient and is definitely stalking me. What do you need? Is it the Triads again? Because I told you, I’m not touching their networks after the ‘unfortunate misunderstanding’ with their payroll server.”

“It’s bigger than the Triads. I need you to decrypt a data chip. And I need to talk to you about Halcyon Industries.”

The line went quiet. Not Finch-quiet, which was normally the space between sentences filled with the background noise of whatever electronic equipment they were running. This was real quiet. Dead air. The kind of quiet that meant I’d just said a word that mattered, a word that shut down all the background processes.

“Where are you?” Finch asked, his voice suddenly stripped of all its manic energy. It was flat, cold, serious.

“My office.”

“Stay there. Don’t leave. Don’t use any networked device. Don’t turn on any screen that connects to anything. Unplug your terminal from the wall. Now. And for the love of everything that isn’t being surveilled, do NOT go near a Halcyon kiosk.”

“Finch—”

“Stay. There. I’ll come to you.” A pause, and I could hear the faint sound of him packing something, the rustle of gear. “And Max? Bring coffee. Real coffee. Not the stuff your drone makes. That stuff is an insult to beans everywhere.”

The line went dead.

I sat in the dark, holding a data chip that might contain nothing or might contain everything. The silence of the room pressed in. I’d rung a bell that couldn’t be un-rung. I’d spoken the name of the beast into a line that was probably compromised, and now the beast knew I was looking.

Outside, Bay Enclave glowed, a beautiful, intricate machine humming with a billion secrets. The kiosks hummed their siren song of immortality. And somewhere in the space between the living and the dead, the stored and the free, the original and the copy, something was being built. Something massive. Something patient.

And I had the feeling—the cold, certain, bone-deep feeling—that I was the last person to the party, and the music had been playing for a very long time.
