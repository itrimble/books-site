Chapter 1: The Glitch

The notification arrived at 3:47 AM, and for a moment, Mira Okonkwo thought the world had finally ended.

Not because of what it said—but because of who sent it. Aldrich Vance had been dead for eleven months. Cancer, the obituary read. Private. Dignified. The kind of death a man like Vance—a founder of FilterFlow, the algorithm that ran beneath every scroll, every search, every quiet hour spent staring into a screen—earned by being too successful to stress about.

The message was simple:

They know what you found. Burn this number.

Mira sat up in the dark, heart hammering against her ribs. Her laptop screen cast a pale blue glow across the bedroom ceiling. On it, the source she'd been cultivating for six weeks—a mid-level engineer at Cascade Technologies, the infrastructure company that quietly powered half of Silicon Valley—had sent a compressed archive thirty minutes earlier. She'd been too tired to open it. Now she was too awake to breathe.

She opened the archive.

The files were clinical: server logs, internal memos, a video recorded on a phone that shook slightly as someone walked down a fluorescent-lit corridor. The video showed a whiteboard covered in diagrams. A woman was speaking off-camera, her voice low and urgent.

"—project codename UNFILTER. If this goes live, every prediction model on the planet becomes a weapon. They can see what you'll do before you decide to do it. Not guess. See. And they're not telling anyone."

Mira played the video twice. Three times. Her mouth went dry.

She was a tech journalist. Twelve years at the San Francisco Chronicle, then four at Wired before going independent. She'd broken the data broker scandal of 2027, the one that got the FTC to actually move for once. She knew what a real story looked like. She knew what a setup looked like too—disinformation, honey pots, engineered leaks from corporate competitors trying to kneecap each other.

This didn't feel like any of those.

She pulled up Cascade's public infrastructure map, cross-referenced the server timestamps from the logs. The data centered on a facility in Boardman, Oregon—a converted paper mill on the banks of the Columbia River, purchased in 2029 through a shell company that traces ultimately to a Cayman entity she'd spent three days untangling last winter. She'd flagged it then as suspicious but filed it under "future story" and moved on.

Now it was 4:12 AM, and she was reading evidence that the future had arrived without press releases.

Her phone buzzed again. A text from the engineer, no number—just a burner, like she'd been told to expect.

Last warning. They're already moving the data. Once it's gone, there's no proof.

Mira typed back: Who are "they"?

The response came instantly, as if the person had been waiting.

Everyone. Start with who owns Cascade. Then ask yourself why the NSA just renewed a contract they won't talk about. Then stop asking questions and disappear for a while. I'm serious.

Please.

That word sat on her screen like an epitaph.

She closed the laptop. Opened it again. Pulled up her secure notes, the ones encrypted with a key she kept on a Yubikey in her sock drawer. She created a new entry: Cascade/UNFILTER — possible behavioral prediction infrastructure, national security implications, source at risk. She dated it, timestamped it, encrypted it.

Then she did something she hadn't done in years.

She called her editor.

The line rang four times before Pete Hanrahan answered, his voice thick with sleep and suspicion. "Mira. It's almost five AM. Your circle." "Pete." She kept her voice flat. Controlled. "I have something. I don't know what yet, but it's real. I'm going to need the Chronicle's legal team on standby, and I need you to trust me for the next forty-eight hours while I figure out if I'm about to break something huge or get myself killed."

A long pause. In it, she could hear a dog bark somewhere in the background of his San Mateo house, the distant hum of a refrigerator.

"Mira. You're scaring me."

"Good. Be scared. Be very scared."

She told him the outline. Not the details—he'd have plausible deniability that way, the kind that kept newspapers out of federal courthouses. Just enough: a source, a facility, something called UNFILTER, and the unmistakable signature of technology that could see around corners of human behavior the way an astronomer reads the light of extinct stars.

When she finished, Pete was quiet for a long time.

"Come in tomorrow. Today. Whatever it is. We'll talk."

"Pete, I can't—"

"Fine. Don't come in. But don't do anything stupid either. And Mira?" His voice dropped. "Whatever you found, it's not worth your life. Nothing is. You got that?"

She hung up without answering.

The sun was starting to come up over the East Bay hills, painting the ceiling in shades of amber and rose. Mira sat in the blue glow of her monitor, the archive still open, the dead man's warning still glowing on her phone, and for the first time in twenty years of journalism, she understood that some stories don't wait for you to be ready.

This one was already running.

She closed her eyes. Counted to ten. Opened them again.

Then she started digging.
