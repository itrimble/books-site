Chapter 1: Welcome Aboard

The camera saw everything first. That was the problem.

The camera saw everything first. That was the problem.

Marcus Cole pressed record as the shuttle bus pulled into Port Canaveral's Cruise Terminal 10, and the red light on his Sony ZV-E10 became the eye through which he'd process the next seven days. Through the viewfinder, the MV Elysium looked like a city tipped on its side — fourteen decks of white steel and tinted glass rising out of the harbor like a glacier calved from some future civilization. Two thousand feet of ship. A hundred and ten thousand gross tons. The kind of vessel that made you understand why ancient peoples invented sea gods: because anything that big shouldn't float, and the fact that it did required either engineering or prayer.

"What's up, Cole's World," he said to the lens, keeping his voice in the register his analytics said performed best — casual, warm, the guy you'd sit next to at a bar. "Your boy is about to do something that travel Twitter said was impossible. I'm about to spend seven nights on a luxury adults-only cruise ship — we're talking premium dining, infinity pools, the works — for one hundred and eighteen dollars a night. That's cheaper than a Holiday Inn Express in Topeka. This is the Ultimate Budget Cruise Challenge, and if I can't do the entire week for under a thousand bucks all-in, I'm donating my camera to a pawn shop."

The woman sitting across the aisle on the shuttle gave him a look — the specific look that said another one — and went back to her phone. Marcus was used to it. Three years into full-time content creation, he'd developed a thick skin about filming in public and a thin skin about everything else.

He'd also developed a growing suspicion that he'd made a terrible mistake leaving his teaching job.

The shuttle doors hissed open, and the Florida humidity hit him like a wet towel. August in Cape Canaveral was not a recommendation — it was a warning. Ninety-three degrees, eighty-seven percent humidity, the kind of weather that made you understand why the Spanish called this place La Florida, the land of flowers, and not La Comfortable, the land of breathable air.

He shouldered his backpack (one bag, always one bag — it was a brand thing and also an anxiety thing) and joined the stream of passengers flowing toward the terminal. The demographics were immediately readable: couples in their thirties and forties (the cruise line's target market), clusters of friends in matching "Birthday Cruise 2028" t-shirts, solo travelers clutching boarding passes with the particular intensity of people who'd booked a vacation they couldn't quite afford. No children. The MV Elysium was adults-only — eighteen and up, no exceptions — which was either a selling point or a red flag, depending on whether you had kids.

Marcus didn't. Not anymore. Not that he ever had, but there'd been a time when Alicia had wanted them, and he'd said "next year" enough times that next year became never and never became divorce papers.

He filmed the check-in process — face ID scan, health screening, the digital wristband that would serve as room key, ship ID, and payment method for the next seven days. The wristband synced to the ship's app, which synced to the ship's AI concierge system, which the cruise line had branded with the kind of grandiose naming convention that only a marketing team could love.

"Welcome aboard, Marcus," said the tablet at the final check-in station. The voice was warm, female, modulated to land somewhere between Siri and a favorite aunt. "I'm Athena. I'll be your personal concierge for the duration of your voyage. Based on your preferences, I've pre-loaded some recommendations. Would you like to hear them?"

"Sure," Marcus said, because content.

"I notice you enjoy early morning workouts — your YouTube upload schedule suggests you're typically active before six AM. The fitness center on Deck 12 opens at five-thirty, and I'd recommend the sunrise yoga class on the bow deck at six. For dining, given your channel's focus on budget-conscious travel, you might be interested to know that all specialty restaurants are included in your fare — no surcharges. I'd particularly recommend the Korean barbecue on Deck 8. And based on your recent viewing history, tonight's comedy show at the Red Room features Danielle Huang, whose special you watched twice last month."

Marcus lowered the camera. "How do you know my viewing history?"

"Your streaming preferences are linked through your social media authentication. It's all in the terms of service." The tablet displayed a pleasant animation of a compass rose. "Shall I continue?"

"I'm good." He picked up the camera again, angling it to show the tablet. "Okay, Cole's World, I'm not going to lie — that was a little creepy. Athena knows what I watch on Netflix. Your cruise ship AI knows your Netflix. Welcome to 2028."

He said it as a joke. It was a joke. Everything was a joke until it wasn't.

The cabin was exactly what one hundred and eighteen dollars a night bought you: a rectangle. One hundred and seventy-two square feet of interior space with no window, no balcony, and no natural light. A queen bed that touched both side walls, a bathroom you could span with outstretched arms, a small desk, and a flat-screen TV mounted at an angle that suggested the installer had given up on geometry.

Marcus loved it. Not because it was good — it was objectively the smallest room he'd stayed in since a hostel bunk in Bangkok — but because it was perfect for content. The contrast between this closet and the ship's public luxury was the entire video premise.

"One hundred and eighteen dollars a night," he said, standing on the bed because there was nowhere else to stand and hold the camera at a useful angle. "And I know what you're thinking — Marcus, you could have gotten a Holiday Inn for that. And you're right. But does the Holiday Inn have six restaurants, an infinity pool, a nightclub, and a tattoo parlor? Because the MV Elysium does. The question isn't whether this cabin is worth the money. The question is whether you ever need to come back to this cabin when you've got" — he gestured expansively at the door, meaning everything beyond it — "all of that."

He reviewed the footage on the flip screen. Decent. The lighting was terrible, but that was fixable in post. He made a note to get B-roll of the suite-level cabins for comparison — he'd seen the pricing online, and the Rockstar Suites went for two thousand a night. Someone was paying seventeen times what he was for the same ocean.

His phone buzzed. Keisha.

How's the floating petri dish?

He typed back: Cabin is smaller than my bathroom. Ship AI knows my Netflix. Think I'm going to die here.

Perfect. Lean into the suffering. Algorithm loves suffering.

That's dark.

That's data. Film everything.

She was right. Keisha was almost always right, which was why she managed his channel and why he sometimes resented her for it. She had the clarity of someone who'd never stood in front of a camera — she saw content where Marcus saw life, saw analytics where he saw people, saw opportunity where he saw... whatever this was. A career. A calling. A thing he did because he couldn't do the other thing anymore.

The other thing being teaching. The other thing being standing in front of thirty-two tenth-graders in Baltimore and watching some of them catch fire with understanding and watching others slip further away and knowing — knowing with absolute certainty — that the system was designed to let them slip. He'd quit because he couldn't save them. He'd started a YouTube channel because — well, he'd told himself it was because he had things to say about travel and budget living and the democratization of experience, but the truth was simpler and uglier: he'd quit being useful and started being watchable.

He shook it off. He was good at shaking things off. It was a survival skill he'd developed in childhood (only child, single mom, West Baltimore — you learned early that dwelling was a luxury) and refined in adulthood (marriage, divorce, career change — you learned again that dwelling was a trap).

The ship's horn sounded — a low, tectonic note that vibrated in his chest. Sail-away.

He grabbed the camera and headed topside.

The pool deck on Deck 12 was the cruise line's showcase, and it showed. Two infinity pools cantilevered over the stern, connected by a shallow wading channel that glowed turquoise in the late afternoon sun. A DJ booth was built into the superstructure, already pumping something that sat at the intersection of house music and Caribbean rhythm. The bar — all polished teak and brushed steel — curved along the port side like a parenthetical, and the bartender working the near end had the practiced ease of someone who'd been pouring drinks on moving surfaces for years.

Marcus worked his way to the rail and pointed the camera at the terminal receding behind them. Port Canaveral shrank with the particular slowness of departure by sea — not the abrupt severance of a plane leaving a gate, but the gradual, almost mournful pulling-away of a ship from shore. The umbilical lines fell away. The gap between hull and dock widened from feet to yards to something that no longer felt bridgeable. The land became a line, then a suggestion, then a memory.

He'd read once that the ancient Greeks had a word for it — nostos, the ache of returning home. But there should have been a word for the other thing, too: the ache of leaving, the moment when the shore lets you go and you realize that you're now subject to the ocean's schedule, not your own.

"You going to film the whole thing, or you going to have a drink?"

Marcus turned. The bartender was watching him from behind the bar — young, maybe mid-twenties, Black, with short twists and a smile that looked both genuine and practiced. His name tag read DEVON.

"Can I do both?"

"Depends on how steady your hands are after the third one." Devon was already reaching for a glass. "What are you having?"

"Whatever's cheapest."

"Everything's included."

"Then whatever's best."

Devon laughed — a real laugh, not a service-industry laugh — and started building something with rum. "You a YouTuber? Influencer?"

"YouTuber. Influencer is a stretch." Marcus set the camera on the bar, lens still running. He'd learned early that the best content happened when people forgot the camera was there. "Budget travel. I'm doing a thing about doing this cruise for cheap."

"On the Elysium?" Devon slid the drink across — something amber and bright with a mint garnish. "Good luck. They built this ship to separate you from money. Every surface is designed to make you forget what things cost."

"Isn't everything included?"

"The drinks, the food, the restaurants. But then there's the spa, the shops, the Wi-Fi packages, the shore excursion upsells. They get you." He wiped the bar with the automatic motion of someone who'd wiped ten thousand bars. "But you know what they really get you on?"

"What?"

"The photos. Ship photographer takes your picture at dinner, at the port, at the shows. Then they put them on screens all over the ship so you see yourself having fun, and you think, 'I should buy that memory.' Twenty bucks a photo, seventy for the package. They sell a quarter million dollars in photos per cruise."

"That's —" Marcus calculated. "That's criminal."

"That's the business model. We sell you your own experience." Devon looked at the camera. "But you already know that. That's your whole job."

Marcus picked up his drink. It was better than it had any right to be. "You're pretty philosophical for a bartender."

"Pre-law degree from the University of Illinois." Devon's smile thinned slightly. "Eighty-seven thousand dollars in debt. Turns out you can't practice law with a bachelor's. So here I am, pouring rum for people who spent less on this vacation than I owe Sallie Mae."

There was a beat of recognition between them — two men in their twenties and thirties, both performing, both trying to figure out if this was the thing or just the thing before the thing. Marcus liked him immediately.

"I'm Marcus."

"Devon. And just so you know — the rum's from Barbados, the mint's from the hydroponic garden on Deck 6, and the juice is canned. Budget-friendly content: tell your audience to drink rum on a cruise, never wine. The wine markup is insane."

"You're good at this."

"At bartending?"

"At content."

Devon's expression shifted — something flickered there, a guard going up or coming down, Marcus couldn't tell. "I'm good at talking to people who are watching. It's a requirement when you grow up on the South Side." He nodded toward the rail, where the shore had vanished entirely. "Welcome aboard, Marcus. Seven days. No kids, no drama, just ocean. Should be fun."

It should have been.
